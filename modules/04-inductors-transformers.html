Inductors & Transformers

The capacitor's magnetic twin: a part that stores energy in a field of its own making, fights any change in current — and bites, hard, if you don't respect it. AoE 3rd ed. §1.5.

Why this matters

Inductors are rarer on a board than capacitors, but they run the show wherever serious power or radio lives: every switching power supply (which is to say, every charger, laptop and phone) works by pumping energy into an inductor and letting it back out; RF circuits tune with them; motors, relays and solenoids are them — a coil is how electricity is turned into pull and spin. And the transformer — two inductors sharing one magnetic field — is the reason the power grid works at all, and the thing standing between the mains and your low-voltage electronics.

There's also a menace side, and H&H are blunt about it. Interrupt the current through an inductor and it responds with a voltage spike of whatever size it takes to keep that current flowing — routinely hundreds of volts from a little 12 V relay coil. That spike is called inductive kick; it is why unprotected switch contacts arc themselves to death and why transistors driving relays die mysteriously. The one-cent fix — a flyback diode — is the first reflex this module installs. Every relay coil gets one. No exceptions.

Physics background

Two facts of electromagnetism, and everything here follows:

1. Current makes a magnetic field (Ampère). Send current through a wire and a magnetic field circles it; wind the wire into a coil and the turns' fields stack into a strong field threading the coil. Wrap the turns around iron or ferrite and the core multiplies the flux hundreds of times over.

2. A changing field makes a voltage (Faraday). If the magnetic flux through a loop changes, a voltage appears around the loop — and by Lenz's law it points in the direction that opposes the change. A coil therefore fights changes in its own current: try to change I and the changing flux induces a back-voltage pushing the other way. The coil's "electrical inertia" is its inductance L, measured in henries (H):

V = L · dI/dt volts across the coil, per amp-per-second of current change; 1 H is a big inductor — real parts run µH to H

Building the field takes work, and the field stores it:

E = ½ L I² joules held in the magnetic field — handed back, on the inductor's terms, when the current falls

Now put V = L·dI/dt next to the capacitor's I = C·dV/dt. Same equation with V and I swapped. The inductor is the capacitor's exact dual: every fact you learned in Module 03 has a mirror-image twin here — swap voltage↔current, C↔L, series↔ parallel, and RC↔L/R. Learn the dictionary once and you get this whole module half price.

V = L·dI/dt, unpacked

Read it the way we read the capacitor law — slowly, because it's the whole component in one line:

  • Steady current → zero voltage. If dI/dt = 0, then V = 0. At settled DC an ideal inductor is a piece of wire. It simply isn't there. (Real ones have winding resistance — more on that below.)
  • Fast change → big voltage. Try to change the current quickly and the coil answers with a proportionally large voltage. Try to change it instantly and it answers with an infinite one — which no real circuit survives politely. Conclusion: the current through an inductor cannot jump.
  • So, instantaneously, an inductor behaves like a current source holding whatever current it currently carries. In the first moment after you throw a switch, an inductor that was carrying nothing is a 0 A source — i.e. an open circuit. Long after, it's a short. Exactly opposite to the capacitor, limit for limit.

In short: a capacitor fights voltage change; an inductor fights current change. Here is the full dictionary — worth a slow minute, because it converts Module 03 into this one line by line:

RL circuits — the same exponential, mirrored

Connect a battery V through a resistor R to an inductor L and close the switch. At the first instant the current is zero and can't jump, so the inductor takes the whole battery voltage and the current starts rising at slope V/L. As current builds, R steals more of the voltage, less is left across L, and the rise slows… a rate of change proportional to the remaining distance. You've met this curve:

I(t) = (V/R) · (1 − e−t/τ)     τ = L/R current climbing toward its DC value V/R — the RC charging curve with I and V swapped

Everything you memorized about τ carries straight over: 63% of the way at 1τ, 86% at 2τ, "done" at 5τ. Only the formula flips — note it's L divided by R now. A small resistance makes a long time constant (nothing to burn the energy in), where for capacitors small R meant fast. And when the drive is removed but a path remains, the current decays the same way: I(t) = I₀·e−t/τ through whatever resistance the loop offers. Hold that thought — "whatever resistance the loop offers" is about to become the most dangerous phrase in Part I.

Inductive kick — the menace

Suppose an inductor is happily carrying current and you open the switch. The current must now fall to zero very fast — dI/dt is huge and negative — and the inductor does exactly what V = L·dI/dt says: it generates whatever voltage it takes to keep its current flowing for another instant. Not the supply voltage. Whatever it takes. The current has nowhere to go except into the few picofarads of stray capacitance around the switch, so the voltage rises until something conducts — an arc across the opening contacts, or the avalanche breakdown of the poor transistor doing the switching.

How big? A useful estimate: the inductor's energy ½LI² sloshes into the stray capacitance's ½CV², giving

Vpeak ≈ I · √(L / Cstray) the LC "surge impedance" estimate; with Cstray ~ 100 pF the multiplier √(L/C) is enormous

Plug in numbers to feel the menace: 100 mA through 100 mH with 100 pF of stray capacitance gives √(L/C) ≈ 31.6 kΩ and Vpeak ≈ 3 kV. From a coil that was running on maybe 12 V. In practice losses and larger real strays tame that to "merely" hundreds of volts — still ample to arc relay contacts (each arc transfers metal and pits them) and far beyond the 40–60 V ratings of ordinary transistors. This isn't a corner case; it's what inductors do, every single time, unless you give the current somewhere to go.

The one-cent fix: the flyback diode

Put a plain diode across the coil, cathode to the positive rail. In normal operation the diode sees the supply voltage backwards and does nothing at all. The instant the switch opens, the coil's terminal voltage flies past the rail — and the diode conducts, giving the current a loop to circulate in. The "kick" is clamped to one diode drop (≈0.6 V) above the supply, and the current dies gently through the coil's own resistance with τ = L/R.

The cost of this politeness: the relay releases a little more slowly, since the current coasts instead of stopping dead. Almost always a fine trade. (When it isn't — fast relays, solenoid valves — a zener or R-C snubber clamps at a higher voltage and kills the current faster. Same idea: give the energy a designated exit.)

Transformers — two coils, one field

Wind two separate coils on the same core and you've built a transformer. The primary (Np turns) is driven with AC; its changing current makes a changing flux in the core; that same changing flux threads the secondary (Ns turns) and — Faraday again — induces a voltage in it. Each turn of either winding embraces the same flux, so each turn gets the same volts. Voltage is therefore doled out per-turn:

Vs / Vp = Ns / Np volts per turn is the same for every winding on the core

A transformer is not a source of free power — it converts. Power in ≈ power out (good ones manage 90–99%), so the currents must transform inversely: step the voltage down 10× and the available current goes up 10×. That's the entire point of the power grid — transmit at high voltage and low current (thin cables, small I²R loss), then transform down at your street.

Combine the two ratios and something useful pops out: a load Z on the secondary draws Vs/Z; seen from the primary that appears as Vp at Ip = Is·(Ns/Np), so the primary "sees" an impedance

Zprimary = (Np/Ns)² · Zload impedance transforms as the turns ratio squared — voltage ratio times inverse current ratio

which makes the transformer an impedance-matching machine — the trick behind coupling a 200 Ω-loving tube amplifier to an 8 Ω speaker, or a 50 Ω antenna to whatever the receiver wants.

Two more properties earn their keep daily. Isolation: the windings are connected only by magnetic field — no copper path — so the secondary side floats free of the mains. That galvanic break is a safety feature you'll learn to treasure. And the fine print: transformers only work on AC. Faraday's law pays out for changing flux only. Feed the primary DC and after the first instant the flux sits constant, the secondary sees nothing — and the primary, being just a low-resistance coil at DC, cheerfully melts. A transformer on DC is a room heater with one trick.

H&H rules of thumb
  • Never open-circuit an inductor carrying current. It will make the voltage needed to keep that current flowing, and it doesn't care what breaks.
  • Every relay coil gets a flyback diode. Cathode to the positive rail. Reflex, not decision.
  • τ = L/R — dual of RC, and note the division: less resistance means a slower current change.
  • Transformer power in ≈ power out. Step voltage down N×, current capability goes up N×; impedance transforms as N². No free energy, ever.
  • At DC an ideal inductor is a wire — real ones are a wire plus winding resistance, which sets the coil current (that's why relay coils are specified in ohms) and quietly turns "ideal short" into "small resistor".
Plumbing analogy

The capacitor was a rubber membrane. The inductor is mass in the pipe: picture a heavy water wheel — a flywheel — that the flow must spin as it passes.

  • Inertia: open the valve and the flow builds gradually — the pressure goes into spinning up the wheel. Once turning, the wheel is barely there (DC: a wire). The stored spin is kinetic energy ½mv², the exact analog of ½LI².
  • It resists changes in FLOW, not pressure. Steady flow: no fight. Change the flow: the flywheel pushes back with a pressure proportional to how fast you're changing it — V = L·dI/dt in brass and water.
  • Inductive kick is real plumbing: slam a valve shut on a long pipe of moving water and the water's momentum piles into the closed valve as a violent pressure spike — water hammer, the bang your pipes make when a washing machine's solenoid valve snaps shut. Plumbers literally install little air-cushion "hammer arrestors" — the plumbing trade's flyback diodes/snubbers — to give the moving mass somewhere soft to spend its energy. Electronics and plumbing converged on the same fix independently.
  • Transformer: two water wheels in separate pipe loops, linked by a belt, with different radii. The belt forces them to a common surface speed, so the big wheel turns slowly with lots of torque and the small one fast with little — pressure and flow trade off exactly as voltage and current do, and belt power in equals belt power out (minus a little friction: core and copper losses). The two water circuits never exchange a drop — that's isolation.

Where the analogy fails: real flywheels have friction and coast down on their own, while a superconducting inductor's current would circulate forever (ordinary ones stop only because of winding resistance — analogy salvaged, partially). And nothing in the plumbing picture explains why a coil's field can induce voltage in a separate, unconnected coil — magnetic coupling through empty space has no everyday hydraulic counterpart; the belt is a confession that we needed a mechanical linkage to fake it.

Check yourself

Design bench

Pencil, paper, and the duality dictionary. Check the box when you've done it and verified against the solution.

A relay coil is specified 12 V, 400 Ω, 100 mH, switched by a small transistor rated for 40 V. (a) What current does the coil draw when energized? (b) Estimate the voltage spike at turn-off with no protection, using the ½LI² → ½CV² model with Cstray = 100 pF — and say what the model leaves out. (c) Add the flyback diode: pick a part, justify its current rating, and state the cost in release time.

Show solution

(a) At DC the inductance is invisible; the winding resistance sets the current: I = 12 V / 400 Ω = 30 mA. Stored energy: ½LI² = ½ × 0.1 × (0.03)² = 45 µJ — sounds tiny, but delivered into picofarads it isn't.

(b) Vpeak = I·√(L/C) = 0.03 × √(0.1 / 10⁻¹⁰) = 0.03 × √(10⁹) = 0.03 × 31,623 ≈ 950 V. Be honest about the model, though: it's a lossless-LC upper bound. Real coils have more stray and inter-winding capacitance than 100 pF, plus core and resistive losses that eat energy during the swing — measured spikes from small relays typically land in the hundreds of volts. The design conclusion survives every refinement of the model: whether it's 300 V or 950 V, the 40 V transistor loses. (It fails by avalanche breakdown, which at least clamps the spike — transistors as sacrificial zeners, briefly.)

(c) A 1N4007 (or any 1N400x) across the coil, cathode to +12 V. Rating logic: the diode must carry the coil current at the moment of turn-off — the current can't jump, so the diode momentarily conducts exactly 30 mA, decaying from there. A 1 A part has ~30× margin; even a small-signal 1N4148 (200 mA) would do here, but the 1N4007's 1000 V reverse rating is comfortable insurance and the part costs a cent. Cost: the current now coasts down with τ = L/R = 0.1/400 = 0.25 ms, so the relay releases roughly a millisecond ("a few τ") later than an unprotected slam. For almost everything, irrelevant.

You need roughly 12 V DC from 230 V mains, via a transformer feeding a bridge rectifier (the bridge costs about two diode drops ≈ 1.2 V off the peak; smoothing comes in a later module). Choose a secondary RMS voltage and the turns ratio. Then answer the tempting shortcut: why not skip the transformer and just use a resistive divider from the mains?

Show solution

Secondary choice: a rectifier charges toward the peak of the sine, not the RMS. Try a 9 V RMS secondary: peak = 9 × √2 ≈ 12.73 V; minus the bridge's ≈1.2 V leaves ≈ 11.5 V — right in the "roughly 12 V" pocket. Turns ratio: Np/Ns = 230/9 ≈ 25.6, call it 26 : 1 (transformer catalogs simply say "230 V : 9 V" and spare you the counting).

Why the divider is a terrible idea, in ascending order of alarm: (1) Power. A divider stiff enough to hold up its output under load carries the load current through its top resistor from 230 V — dropping ~218 V at, say, 100 mA is 22 W of pure heat, per Thévenin (Module 02) it's a dreadful source besides. The transformer converts instead of burning: power in ≈ power out. (2) Regulation. The divider's output collapses with load; the transformer's barely sags. (3) Safety — the real disqualifier. A divider is copper-connected to the mains: its "12 V" output can sit 230 V-worth of shock above earth, and one failed resistor puts the full mains on your circuit and its user. The transformer's magnetic-only coupling isolates the secondary completely. You can touch it. That's not a convenience; it's the difference between an appliance and an incident.

A small amplifier delivers its rated power only into a ≈200 Ω load, but your speaker is 8 Ω. Choose the turns ratio of a matching transformer, and verify numerically that the amplifier actually sees ≈200 Ω.

Show solution

Impedance transforms as the turns ratio squared, so pick Np/Ns = √(200/8) = √25 = 5 : 1 — primary (amplifier side) has 5× the turns.

Verify forward: Zprimary = (Np/Ns)² × 8 = 25 × 8 = 200 Ω ✓. Verify from first principles: put 10 V RMS on the primary. Secondary: 10/5 = 2 V across 8 Ω → Is = 0.25 A. Primary current: 0.25/5 = 50 mA. The amp sees 10 V/0.05 A = 200 Ω ✓ — and power checks too: 2 V × 0.25 A = 10 V × 0.05 A = 0.5 W on both sides, as conservation insists. This is exactly how tube amplifiers (and door-entry intercoms, and old telephone networks) married high-impedance electronics to low-impedance transducers.
