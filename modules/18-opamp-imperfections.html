Op-Amp Imperfections & Precision Design

The golden rules are a beautiful lie. Here is the itemized bill for believing them — offset, bias current, gain-bandwidth, slew rate — and how to design so the bill stays small. AoE 3rd ed. §4.4 & Ch. 5.

Why this matters

The golden rules — the inputs draw no current, the output does whatever keeps the inputs equal — are a beautiful lie. At a gain of 5, amplifying volts, nobody ever catches the lie: the errors hide three decimal places down and the circuit on your bench matches the circuit in your head. But raise the gain to 1000, or shrink the signal to microvolts, or push the frequency up, and every broken promise sends an invoice. The inputs do draw current, and it makes voltage across every resistor it touches. The inputs are not equal even when the loop is happy — they disagree by a millivolt or so, and your circuit amplifies the disagreement along with the signal. The gain is not infinite, and above a few tens of hertz it isn't even large. This module is the bill, itemized. The good news — and it is genuinely good — is that every line item is specified on the datasheet, so you can total the bill before building anything. That habit, the error budget, is what separates precision design from hopeful soldering, and it's the skill Chapter 5 of H&H exists to teach.

Physics background — where each imperfection comes from

Offset voltage is Module 11's differential pair failing to be perfectly symmetric. The op-amp's front end is two transistors that are supposed to be identical twins; same-die fabrication makes them astonishingly alike — but not exactly alike. A fraction of a percent of mismatch in VBE or Vth means the pair balances with its inputs a millivolt or so apart instead of exactly together. From outside, the amp behaves as if a tiny battery were hidden in series with one input. Precision parts get there by laser-trimming the mismatch at the factory; chopper amps measure and subtract it continuously.

Bias current exists because the diff pair's transistors must be biased on, and that current has to come from somewhere — namely, out of (or into) your circuit through the input pins. BJT inputs need base current: nanoamps to microamps (Module 11's IC/β). FET inputs (Module 12) need only gate leakage: picoamps or less. The golden rule "inputs draw no current" is really "inputs draw Ibias, which you may or may not be able to ignore."

Finite gain-bandwidth is deliberate. A raw multi-stage amplifier with gain of a million would oscillate the instant you closed feedback around it, so designers add an internal compensation capacitor that rolls the open-loop gain off at 6 dB/octave — Module 05's single-RC slope — starting from just a few hertz. The gain is a million at DC and falls all the way to 1 at a megahertz or so. Stability is bought with bandwidth, on purpose.

Slew rate is that same compensation capacitor's other tax. To move the output, the input stage must charge the capacitor, and the most current it can supply is Module 11's tail current. Maximum dV/dt = Itail/Ccomp — a hard speed limit on the output, no matter how loudly the input demands more.

Offset voltage Vos — the inputs that don't quite agree

Model it as a tiny battery in series with one input: the op-amp servos its inputs to be equal plus Vos. The feedback network can't tell that battery from signal, so it gets amplified by the full noise gain of the circuit:

Vout,error = Vos × (1 + Rf/Rg) the noise gain — equal to the closed-loop gain of a non-inverting amp; note an inverting amp of gain −10 still has a noise gain of 11

Magnitudes to memorize: a jellybean op-amp has Vos of 1–5 mV; a trimmed precision part like the OP07, ~25 µV; an auto-zero (chopper) amp, ~1 µV. At gain 5, even 5 mV of offset makes 25 mV of output error — who cares. At gain 1000 it makes 5 volts, which may be the entire output range. And offset isn't a constant you can trim once and forget: it drifts with temperature — a few µV/°C for ordinary parts, ~0.5 µV/°C for precision ones — so a pot that nulls the offset at 25 °C is wrong again by lunchtime. For serious DC work, drift is the spec that matters more than the offset itself.

Bias & offset current — the inputs that do draw current

Each input sources or sinks Ibias continuously. That current flows through whatever resistance the input sees — your source resistance, your feedback network — and by Module 01's oldest law it makes a voltage: Verror = Ibias × Rsource, indistinguishable from signal and amplified by the noise gain like everything else. At microscale the arithmetic is merciless: 80 nA (a classic BJT input) through a 10 MΩ pH electrode is 0.8 V of phantom "signal" before you've measured anything.

Two defenses. The classic trick: put a resistor equal to Rf∥Rg in series with the + input, so both inputs see the same resistance. The two bias currents then generate equal common-mode voltages that cancel, leaving only the error from the offset current Ios — the mismatch between the two bias currents, typically 5–10× smaller. The modern defense: when source impedances are high, stop fighting and use a FET-input part (Module 12) whose picoamp bias currents make the whole error budget line vanish. A 50 pA TL072 on that same pH electrode: 0.5 mV. Rule of thumb — above ~100 kΩ of source impedance, BJT inputs start needing excuses; above 1 MΩ, just use the FET.

Gain-bandwidth product — the eternal surprise

Open the datasheet's open-loop gain plot: a flat shelf of ~120 dB below about 10 Hz, then a straight 6 dB/octave descent (Module 05's slope, drawn by the compensation cap) all the way down to gain 1. On that slope, gain × frequency is constant — that constant is the gain-bandwidth product, GBW. Close feedback around the amp and your closed-loop gain holds only as long as the open-loop gain has excess to spare; the closed-loop response corners exactly where your gain line intersects the open-loop rolloff:

fclosed = GBW / Gnoise closed-loop −3 dB bandwidth; G × f = GBW is conserved — you trade one for the other, linearly

So a gain-100 amplifier built on a 1 MHz op-amp has 10 kHz of bandwidth. Not 1 MHz — 10 kHz. This ambushes everyone once: the part is "a megahertz op-amp," the signal is 40 kHz, the gain is 100, and the bench stubbornly shows a rolled-off, phase-shifted mess. Need gain 100 and 100 kHz? You need 10 MHz of GBW, or two gain-of-10 stages (each needing only 1 MHz — cascading is cheaper than heroism).

Slew rate — the output can only walk so fast

Bandwidth limiting is a linear, small-signal effect: the output is still a sine, just smaller and later. Slew limiting is different in kind — a hard ceiling on dV/dt of the output, set by the tail current charging the compensation cap. Ask for an output steeper than SR and the amp simply ramps at SR, turning your sine into a triangle. The steepest slope of a sine A·sin(2πft) is 2πfA, so the amp stays honest only while 2πfA < SR:

fmax = SR / (2πA) full-power bandwidth for output amplitude A (half the peak-to-peak swing)

Keep the two limits distinct, because they scale differently. Small-signal bandwidth (GBW/G) doesn't care about amplitude; slew limiting doesn't care about gain. A 741 (0.5 V/µs) at unity gain has ~1 MHz of small-signal bandwidth, yet a 10 Vpp sine starts triangulating at just 16 kHz — barely past the audio band. Millivolt signals sail through at frequencies where full-swing signals shatter. When a big output looks distorted but a small one doesn't, at the same frequency, that's slew limiting's signature — and it's distortion, not attenuation, so you can't fix it downstream.

Output & supply realities

The golden-rule output drives any load to any voltage; the real one does neither. Classic bipolar output stages stop 1.5–2 V short of each rail — a 741 on ±15 V swings about ±13 V, and on a single 5 V supply it is nearly useless. Modern rail-to-rail CMOS outputs get within millivolts of the rails (at light load), which is why they own the low-voltage world. Output current is limited too — typically ±10–40 mA, often by deliberate short-circuit protection — so heavy loads (headphones, cables, LEDs) need a buffer stage, however perfect the voltage looks unloaded.

Two more specs in one paragraph: PSRR (power-supply rejection) says how much of the supply's noise leaks in, disguised as input signal — great at DC, but it degrades with frequency along the same 6 dB/octave slope, which is why switching-supply hash at 100 kHz walks straight through an amp that rejects ripple at 100 Hz. CMRR (common-mode rejection) says how well the amp ignores voltage common to both inputs; finite CMRR turns a swinging common-mode level into a small phantom differential input — it behaves like an offset that depends on where your signal sits. Both are quoted in dB; both deserve a glance whenever supplies are dirty or signals ride on a moving pedestal.

Choosing an op-amp — what actually matters for your job

There are thousands of op-amps, but only a handful of reasons to pick one. Identify which imperfection dominates your error budget, and the field collapses:

H&H rules of thumb
  • Error budget before breadboard. Total the DC bill: Vos × noise gain, plus Ibias × (each resistance it flows through) × noise gain. Compare against your signal at the output. If the errors aren't comfortably smaller, change the part or the topology — solder fixes neither.
  • Bandwidth = GBW / noise gain. Every time. Gain 100 on a 1 MHz part is a 10 kHz amplifier. Need both gain and bandwidth? Cascade stages.
  • Check slew at full amplitude: the output is honest only while 2πfA < SR. Big signals hit the wall long before small ones.
  • The "typical" column is marketing. Design to "max." Typical describes the parts the fab is proud of; max is the only number the manufacturer promises. A design that needs typical luck fails in production, one unit at a time.
  • When in doubt: chopper for DC, FET for high impedance. Microvolt offsets and picoamp inputs are cheap now — buying the right silicon beats trimming the wrong silicon.
The employee analogy

The golden-rules op-amp is a perfect employee: infinitely diligent, works for free, instantly does whatever keeps the two inputs matched. The real op-amp is a good employee with quirks you learn to manage:

  • Offset voltage — a systematic bias in their judgment. They consistently report every comparison a millivolt off, the same direction every time. Manageable: calibrate it out, hire a pre-calibrated specialist (precision part), or hire one who re-checks their own bias every millisecond (chopper). But beware — the bias shifts with the office temperature.
  • Bias current — they need a small retainer just to listen. Every consultation costs a trickle of current, and if your question comes through a long thin channel (high source impedance) the retainer distorts the question itself. Keep the channels low-impedance, or engage the FET consultant, who works for picoamps.
  • Gain-bandwidth — brilliant on slow tasks, thinly spread on fast ones. They have a fixed pool of attention: gain × speed = constant. Ask for 1000× scrutiny and you'll get it — on anything slower than a kilohertz. Ask for speed, and accept 10× scrutiny.
  • Slew rate — they can only walk so fast, however urgent the memo. Big changes take proportionally long to deliver, and during the sprint they ignore everything else — including feedback.

Where the analogy fails, delightfully: unlike human quirks, op-amp errors are beautifully specified and repeatable. The datasheet is an honest HR file — every flaw quantified, bounded, and guaranteed. No employee comes with one; every op-amp does. Precision design is just reading it before you delegate.

Check yourself

Design bench

Pencil, paper, and a datasheet's worth of numbers. Total the error budget first, then check the box when you've verified against the solution.

A type-K thermocouple produces about 40 µV/°C, and its wire resistance is a comfortable ~50 Ω. You want 10 mV/°C at the output — gain 250 — with total errors below ±1 °C. Translate the spec into an input-referred error budget, then audit three candidates: the 741, the OP07, and a 1 µV auto-zero chopper. Who passes? Don't forget drift.

Show solution

The budget. ±1 °C × 40 µV/°C = the total input-referred error must stay under 40 µV. Bias current barely matters here — 50 Ω of source resistance turns even 80 nA into only 4 µV — so this budget is a pure Vos shootout:

PartVosI_b·R_sAs temperatureVerdict
7412 mV4 µV≈ 50 °CFails by ~50×
OP0725 µV0.05 µV≈ 0.6 °CMarginal pass
Chopper1 µV~0≈ 0.03 °CComfortable win

The 741's 2 mV offset reads as a fifty-degree fever on a sensor that speaks in microvolts — no amount of gain accuracy rescues it. The OP07's 25 µV ≈ 0.6 °C squeaks under the 40 µV line, but check drift: at ~0.5 µV/°C of ambient drift, a 20 °C room-temperature swing adds another 10 µV (0.25 °C) — still passing, with little margin. The chopper's ~1 µV offset and ~0.02 µV/°C drift make the error budget a rounding error, which is exactly why auto-zero amps own thermocouple front ends.

Bandwidth sanity check: gain 250 on the OP07's 0.6 MHz leaves 2.4 kHz — luxurious for a sensor that changes over seconds. DC precision and speed are separate purchases, and here you only need one.

Module 15's transimpedance amplifier, pushed to the extreme: a photodiode delivering single-picoamp currents into a 1 GΩ feedback resistor (1 pA → 1 mV out). Audit the same op-amp archetypes for the one spec that now dominates everything — and then explain why the circuit board itself joins the error budget.

Show solution

Bias current is the whole story. The amp's inverting input sits directly on the summing node, so every ampere of Ibias flows through the 1 GΩ feedback resistor exactly like signal: Verror = Ibias × 1 GΩ. The 741's 80 nA "wants" to be 80 volts — the output slams into the rail before any light arrives. Even the precision OP07 at 1 nA makes 1 V of error — a thousand times the 1 mV signal; its heroic 25 µV offset is utterly irrelevant here. The TL072's 50 pA still makes 50 mV, fifty signals' worth. You need the pA-or-below CMOS/electrometer class (1 pA → 1 mV, and femtoamp parts exist for exactly this job). Different job, different dominant spec — the best op-amp of Task 1 is among the worst here.

The board is a component now. A trace at 15 V sitting near the summing node, across even a teraohm of flux residue and fiberglass leakage, injects 15 pA — more error than the op-amp you so carefully chose. Hence the classic defenses: clean the flux, and surround the summing node with a driven guard ring — a trace held at the same potential, so the leakage path sees zero volts and therefore carries zero current. At gigohm impedances, layout is not packaging; it's circuit design.

Gain of 2, flat to 20 kHz, 6 Vpp output into a 10 kΩ line input. Compute the GBW and slew rate actually required, and judge how exotic a part you need. Then the twist: the same signal into 32 Ω headphones — which spec suddenly rules?

Show solution

GBW: gain 2 to 20 kHz needs 40 kHz of raw GBW; take 10× margin for flatness and low distortion and you want ~0.4–2 MHz — the 1970s already delivered this. Slew: 6 Vpp is A = 3 V, so SR > 2π·20 kHz·3 V ≈ 0.38 V/µs. A TL072 (13 V/µs) has 34× margin; even the ancient 741 technically clears it (0.5 V/µs, uncomfortably close — "typ," remember, so a real design says no). The lesson: audio is easy. Line level into 10 kΩ stresses no modern spec — don't pay for a chopper's offset or a video part's GBW to move 3 V at 20 kHz. Spend the budget on noise, the one spec audio actually taxes.

The twist: 6 Vpp into 32 Ω demands a peak current of 3 V / 32 Ω ≈ 94 mA — several times the ±20–40 mA short-circuit limit of a garden-variety op-amp. The voltage specs all still pass; the part simply cannot push the amperes, and the output clips against its own current limiter. The dominant spec flipped again, this time to output current: use a headphone-driver part, parallel buffers, or an op-amp + push-pull booster inside the loop (Module 09's follower, promoted to power duty). Every load re-asks the question "which imperfection dominates?" — precision design is just never assuming the answer.
