Diodes & Diode Circuits

Your first one-way street — and your first component whose I–V curve refuses to be a straight line. Rectifiers, clamps, and the useful art of breaking down on purpose. AoE 3rd ed. §1.6.

Why this matters

Everything so far — resistors, capacitors, inductors — has been linear: double the voltage, double the current (or its derivative). Linear circuits can filter, divide, and differentiate, but they can never decide. They cannot say "current may pass this way but not that way." The diode is the door out of linear-land, and it's the door everything interesting walks through: rectification is why your wall adapter turns AC into DC, clamp diodes are quietly protecting every input pin you will ever design, and the diode's exponential law is a preview of the transistor — which is, at heart, a diode with ambition. Learn this one nonlinear curve well and Part II will feel like meeting an old friend.

Physics background — the PN junction

Pure silicon is a lousy conductor: its electrons are locked into bonds. Dope it with a whiff of phosphorus (five outer electrons where silicon wants four) and each dopant atom donates a spare electron free to wander — n-type material. Dope with boron (three outer electrons) and each atom leaves a bond one electron short — a mobile hole that behaves like a positive carrier — p-type. Join the two and something remarkable happens at the boundary: electrons from the n side diffuse across to fill holes on the p side, leaving behind a thin zone stripped of mobile carriers — the depletion region — with fixed charged dopant ions on each side. Those fixed charges build an electric field that opposes further diffusion, and the tug-of-war settles at a built-in barrier potential of about 0.6 V in silicon.

Apply external voltage with + on the p side (forward bias) and you push carriers toward the junction, narrowing the depletion region; the fraction of carriers energetic enough to cross the shrinking barrier grows exponentially with voltage, because carrier energies follow a Boltzmann distribution. Reverse the polarity and you fatten the depletion region: only a trickle of thermally generated carriers leaks across. That asymmetry is the whole device, and it's captured by the Shockley equation:

I = IS (eV/nVT − 1)     VT = kT/q ≈ 25 mV at room temperature IS is a tiny leakage scale (~pA); n is an "ideality factor" between 1 and 2

One consequence worth tattooing somewhere: IS grows rapidly with temperature, so at fixed current the forward drop falls by about 2 mV for every °C of warming. That −2 mV/°C is a nuisance (bias points drift), a free thermometer (many temperature sensors are exactly this), and a genuine hazard (a hot diode drops less, hogs more current, gets hotter…). It will follow you through the entire book.

The model ladder — three diodes for the price of one

H&H's habit, worth stealing: keep several models of the same part on a mental ladder, and always use the crudest one the problem tolerates. Climbing the ladder buys accuracy and costs effort; most of the time the bottom rungs are all you need.

(a) The ideal one-way valve

Current flows freely in the direction of the arrow (anode → cathode); nothing flows backward. No forward drop at all. This model answers topology questions: which way does the current go, which diodes in the bridge conduct on this half-cycle, does this clamp ever engage? For figuring out what a diode circuit does, start here — always.

(b) Valve + 0.6 V drop

Same one-way valve, but conducting costs a fixed 0.6 V (0.7 V if it's working hard, 0.3 V for Schottky). This is the workhorse model — it answers virtually every numeric question in practical design: LED resistor values, rectifier output voltages, clamp thresholds. Nine problems out of ten end on this rung.

(c) The exponential truth

The full Shockley curve. You climb up here only when the shape of the curve matters: precision circuits, log converters, understanding why transistors amplify. It's also the rung that explains why rung (b) works at all — which brings us to the key fact.

Why "0.6 V" is such a good lie

An exponential is brutally steep. With VT ≈ 25 mV, the forward drop grows by only

ΔV ≈ 60 mV per decade of current (VT·ln 10 ≈ 58 mV for n = 1 — call it 60 and move on)

Run the numbers: if a diode drops 0.52 V at 1 mA, it drops about 0.58 V at 10 mA and 0.63 V at 100 mA. The current changed by a factor of a hundred; the voltage moved 0.11 V. Flip that around and it's the punchline of the whole section: over any current range you're likely to care about, the diode drop is 0.6–0.7 V, full stop. That's why the crude model (b) is so trustworthy — the exponential's very steepness makes the voltage nearly constant. The same steepness makes the diode useless as a current-setting device: try to set current by picking a voltage and a 60 mV error becomes a 10× current error. Voltage across a diode: predictable. Current from a voltage: a resistor's job, never a diode's.

Plumbing analogy — the check valve

A diode is a spring-loaded flapper valve in the pipe — a check valve. Push water the right way and nothing happens until the pressure reaches the spring's preload (~0.6 V worth); then the flap cracks open and flow is nearly unrestricted, with the valve eating roughly that same small pressure regardless of how hard the water gushes. Push the wrong way and the back-pressure slams the flap onto its seat: no flow, and the harder you push, the tighter it seals.

The zener is the boiler-room cousin: a pressure-relief valve deliberately installed backwards. It stays sealed against reverse pressure up to a factory-set limit (VZ), then opens smoothly and holds the pressure right at that limit no matter how much flow it must pass. Not a failure — a calibrated safety feature.

Where the analogy fails: real diodes leak a little backward (nA of reverse current, where a good flapper seals absolutely); the exponential turn-on is nothing like a mechanical spring's abrupt crack; and reverse breakdown in an ordinary diode isn't the graceful, reusable event the relief valve suggests — exceed a rectifier's rated reverse voltage without limiting the current and you get a one-time-use diode.

Rectifiers — turning AC into DC

The diode's first job, historically and practically: your wall wants to sell you AC, your circuits want to eat DC. The simplest converter is the half-wave rectifier — one diode in series with the load. On positive half-cycles the diode conducts (minus its 0.6 V toll) and the load sees the waveform; on negative half-cycles it blocks and the load sees nothing. You get pulsating DC, but you've thrown away half the input, and whatever smoothing you add must coast through a whole cycle between top-ups.

The full-wave bridge fixes that with four diodes arranged in a diamond. On each half-cycle, one diagonal pair conducts and steers the current through the load in the same direction. Trace it with model (a) and you'll see the routing; switch to model (b) for the price: the current always crosses two diodes in series, so the bridge costs two diode drops — 1.2 to 1.4 V off the peak. (At 12 V that's a shrug; in a 3.3 V design it's a catastrophe, which is why low-voltage supplies use synchronous rectifiers — transistors doing a diode's job, a Part II story.)

Raw rectified output still touches zero a hundred times a second. The fix is a smoothing (reservoir) capacitor across the load: it charges to the peak, then supplies the load by itself while the input sine is below it, sagging until the next peak recharges it. The sag is the ripple, and the estimate is pure Module 03: a cap delivering current I for time Δt droops by

ΔV ≈ I · Δt / C full-wave at 50 Hz: peaks arrive every Δt = 1/(2·50 Hz) = 10 ms; half-wave: 20 ms

Note the pessimism baked in: it assumes the cap carries the load for the whole interval at constant current, when really the recharge arrives a bit early. Good — power-supply arithmetic should err on the side of more capacitor. And notice the half-wave penalty is now concrete: double the Δt, double the ripple, for the same C.

Clamps & protection — the diode as bodyguard

Every IC input pin has an allowed voltage window, usually "a diode drop outside the supply rails." Stray outside it — static discharge, a sensor on a long cable, a probe slip — and you can latch up or destroy the chip. The classic defense is a pair of clamp diodes: one from the pin up to the positive rail, one from ground up to the pin. Inside the window both are reverse-biased and invisible. Let the input try to exceed the rail by 0.6 V and the top diode conducts, dumping the excess into the supply; dive below ground and the bottom one catches it. The pin simply cannot leave the range −0.6 V to VCC+0.6 V. (Most chips ship with exactly these diodes built in — small, fragile ones.)

But a clamp diode without a series resistor is a promise it can't keep. The diode clamps voltage; something must limit the current, or the offending source will happily push amps through a part rated for milliamps. The series R turns "a 12 V fault into a 3.9 V node" problem into "a 12 V fault into a resistor" problem — and resistors are cheap and stoic. Rule: never let an input pin meet the outside world without a series resistor in front of its clamps.

The same reflex catches inductive kick, which you met in Module 04: interrupt current through a relay coil and the inductor generates whatever voltage it takes to keep its current flowing — hundreds of volts, if you let it. A flyback diode across the coil (reverse-biased in normal operation) gives that current a polite one-way exit loop, clamping the spike to a diode drop above the supply. One diode, one destroyed-transistor mechanism eliminated.

Zener diodes — breakdown on purpose

Push any diode's reverse voltage far enough and it breaks down: the junction field gets strong enough to rip carriers loose (or tunnel them across), and reverse current rises almost vertically. In a rectifier this is a place you never go. A zener diode is built to go there for a living: doped so breakdown happens at a chosen, tightly specified voltage VZ — 3.3 V, 5.1 V, 12 V, pick from the catalog — and the junction survives indefinitely as long as you limit the current and respect the power rating. A nearly vertical I–V line is a nearly constant voltage over a wide current range: a voltage reference.

The minimal circuit is the shunt regulator: series resistor from the raw supply, zener from the output node to ground, load across the zener. The resistor drops the difference; the zener sits at VZ and conducts whatever current the load doesn't take:

R = (Vin − VZ) / (Izener + Iload) keep a few mA in the zener at worst case, or it drifts off its knee and regulation dies

Its limits are worth naming now (Module 20 fixes them): the total current is constant, so the zener must burn whatever the load doesn't — maximum waste at zero load; the output impedance is the zener's slope resistance (ohms to tens of ohms), not the milliohms a real supply needs; and VZ drifts with temperature. It's a fine reference and a poor power supply. Real regulators wrap an amplifier around a reference exactly to fix these three complaints.

A field guide to the diode family

Same physics, different tuning. What the drawer labels actually mean:

Type (example)Forward dropSpeedReach for it when…
Small-signal silicon (1N4148)0.6–0.7 V~4 ns signal steering, clamps, logic — anything under ~100 mA
Rectifier (1N4007)0.7–1 V at ampsslow (µs recovery) 50/60 Hz power rectification; rugged, cheap, unhurried
Schottky (1N5819)0.3–0.4 Vvery fast (no stored charge) low-drop rectification, fast switching — but leakier in reverse
Zener (1N4733, 5.1 V)0.6 V forward; VZ reverse— references, clamps at voltages other than 0.6 V, protection
LED1.8–3.3 V by color (red lowest, blue/white highest)— light. Still a diode: same exponential, same need for a series resistor
H&H rules of thumb
  • Silicon diode drop ≈ 0.6 V (0.7 V working hard; 0.3 V for Schottky). Use it and stop worrying.
  • The drop moves −2 mV/°C — bias drift, free thermometer, thermal-runaway hazard, all in one number.
  • The exponential means ~60 mV per decade of current: the drop is "constant" precisely because the curve is so steep.
  • A bridge rectifier costs two drops: 1.2–1.4 V off the peak.
  • Reservoir-cap ripple: ΔV ≈ I·Δt/C, with Δt = 10 ms for full-wave at 50 Hz (8.3 ms at 60 Hz; double for half-wave).
  • Never let an input pin see fault current without a series resistor — clamp diodes limit voltage; only resistors limit current.

Check yourself

Design bench

Pencil, paper, and the 0.6 V model — climb the ladder only where a task forces you to. Check the box when you've worked it and compared with the solution.

A transformer gives you 9 V RMS. Design the rest: full-wave bridge, reservoir capacitor, and a load that draws 200 mA. Compute the peak voltage, the DC output, and choose a standard capacitor that keeps ripple under 1 V peak-to-peak at 50 Hz.

Show solution

Peak: 9 V RMS × √2 = 12.7 V. The bridge takes two diode drops: 12.7 − 1.2 ≈ 11.5 V DC output (at the ripple's top; the average sits half a ripple lower).

Capacitor from ΔV = I·Δt/C, rearranged: C = I·Δt/ΔV = 0.2 A × 0.01 s / 1 V = 2000 µF. (Δt = 10 ms because full-wave at 50 Hz delivers a peak every half-cycle.) Nearest standard value up — ripple specs round up — is 2200 µF, giving ΔV ≈ 0.2×0.01/2.2×10⁻³ ≈ 0.91 V. The simulation above, set to 9 V/2200 µF/57 Ω, shows ~0.8 V — the formula's pessimism at work, as promised.

Habit check: the cap must be rated for the peak (12.7 V, so a 16 V or 25 V part), and the diodes see roughly the full peak in reverse — a 1N4007 yawns.

An ADC input runs on 0–3.3 V rails, protected by clamp diodes to each rail. The sensor feeding it lives on a long cable and can fault to ±12 V. Choose a series resistor that keeps the clamp current under 1 mA in the worst case, then discuss what that resistor costs you at the ADC's sampling input.

Show solution

Positive fault: the top clamp engages at 3.3 + 0.6 = 3.9 V, so the resistor absorbs 12 − 3.9 = 8.1 V. For under 1 mA: R ≥ 8.1 V / 1 mA = 8.1 kΩ → standard 10 kΩ gives 0.81 mA. Now the habit that separates designs that survive from ones that don't: check the other polarity. At −12 V the bottom clamp holds the pin at −0.6 V and the resistor absorbs 11.4 V → 1.14 mA through 10 kΩ. A hair over the 1 mA target — fine if the datasheet's injection-current limit is the typical "±2 mA", but if it says 1 mA and means it, bump to 12 kΩ (0.95 mA worst case).

The cost: an ADC's sample-and-hold charges a small internal cap through your resistor, and its input looks like a finite impedance during conversion. 10 kΩ against a typical spec of "source impedance ≤ 10 kΩ at full speed" is right at the edge — you may need to slow the sampling, add a small cap at the pin to feed the sample-and-hold locally, or buffer. Protection versus signal integrity is a genuine trade, not a checkbox.

Make ~5.1 V from a 12 V supply for a load that draws 10 mA, using a 5.1 V zener. Choose the series resistor so the zener keeps at least 5 mA with the load connected, compute the worst-case zener dissipation, and name the architecture's built-in weakness.

Show solution

The resistor must pass load + zener minimum: 10 + 5 = 15 mA, dropping 12 − 5.1 = 6.9 V. R = 6.9 V / 15 mA = 460 Ω → standard 470 Ω (14.7 mA total, leaving the zener 4.7 mA under load — a whisker under target; the knee doesn't fall off a cliff at 5.0 mA, but if you're strict, round down to 430 Ω, because here more current is the safe direction).

Worst case for the zener is the load disconnected: it must then swallow the whole 14.7 mA, dissipating 14.7 mA × 5.1 V ≈ 75 mW — comfortable for a 400 mW part. (The resistor burns 6.9²/470 ≈ 100 mW, always — spec it ¼ W.)

The weakness: the input current is ~15 mA forever, whether the load wants 10 mA or nothing — the zener just burns the leftovers. Add sloppy regulation (the zener's slope resistance) and temperature drift, and you have a circuit that's a fine reference for a microamp-hungry comparator and a lousy power supply. Module 20 wraps feedback around this exact idea and fixes all three flaws.
