Emitter Followers & Current Sources

A transistor circuit with a voltage gain of one sounds useless — until you notice what it does to impedance. Plus the circuit that holds a current steady no matter what you hang on it. AoE 3rd ed. §2.2–2.3.

Why this matters

The emitter follower has a voltage gain of one. The output is a faithful copy of the input, minus a diode drop. And yet it is everywhere — inside op-amps, at the output of every audio amplifier, buffering every reference voltage that has real work to do. Why would anyone build an amplifier that doesn't amplify? Because voltage was never the problem. Module 02 ended with a sensor whose signal collapsed to 9% the moment a load touched it — not because the voltage was too small, but because the source was too weak. What the follower transforms is impedance: it takes a feeble, high-impedance source and re-issues the same voltage from a stiff, low-impedance one. Module 02 taught you why stiff sources matter; this module finally builds one.

The second half of the module is the other great trick a transistor plays: the current source — a two-terminal-ish circuit that pushes a fixed current through whatever you connect, the exact dual of the voltage sources we've leaned on so far. Current sources are analog design's other plumbing: they bias amplifiers, drive LEDs, charge capacitors into clean ramps, and (as "active loads") quietly make most of the gain inside every op-amp you'll ever use.

Physics background — why the emitter follows

Module 08 established the transistor rules: the base–emitter junction is a diode, and once it conducts, VBE sits pinned near 0.6 V — the exponential diode law means large swings of collector current cost only tens of millivolts of VBE. So if the base moves, the emitter has no choice: with VBE effectively constant, the emitter tracks the base, 0.6 V below it. Wire the collector straight to the positive rail, put a resistor RE from emitter to ground, drive the base — that's the whole circuit.

There is feedback hiding inside, and it's worth seeing because it previews every feedback circuit to come. Suppose a load tugs the emitter downward. The base hasn't moved, so VBE = VB − VE grows — and collector current rises exponentially with VBE, so the transistor pulls up harder, lifting the emitter right back. The circuit actively fights any attempt to move its output. That's why the output is stiff: not because the transistor is strong, but because it corrects.

Now the impedance arithmetic, gently. Base current is emitter current divided by (β + 1) — the transistor supplies the rest from the collector rail. So if a load draws ΔI from the emitter, the source driving the base only has to supply ΔI/(β+1): the base sees the load magnified ×(β+1), roughly Zin ≈ β·Zload. Run the same argument backwards: any resistance Rsource in the base circuit only carries base current, so from the emitter's side it looks (β+1)× smaller: the load sees the source shrunk ÷(β+1). One transistor, and impedances are transformed by a factor of a hundred in whichever direction helps you.

The emitter follower

Collector to the positive rail, RE from emitter to ground, signal into the base. The DC story is one line:

Vout = Vin − 0.6 V     gain ≈ 1 the emitter follows the base, one diode drop below — hence the name

The impedance story is where the value lives:

Zin ≈ β · (RE ∥ Rload)      Zout ≈ Rsource/β + re the base sees the load ×(β+1); the load sees the source ÷(β+1), plus a little re

What is that re? Honesty time. Even with a perfectly stiff source driving the base, the emitter isn't a perfect voltage source, because VBE isn't perfectly constant — it creeps slightly as current changes, and the diode law says exactly how much. The slope works out to a beautifully simple effective resistance in series with the emitter, the intrinsic emitter resistance, "the little re":

re = 25 mV / IC 25 Ω at 1 mA, 2.5 Ω at 10 mA — a real, physical resistance you never soldered in

It isn't a component; it's the diode law differentiated (the 25 mV is kT/q, thermal physics, the same for every silicon transistor ever made). At 1 mA it's 25 Ω, and it sets the floor on how stiff a follower's output can get: however large β is, Zout never drops below re. Run more current, get a stiffer output — one of the recurring bargains of analog design. The little re will matter even more in Module 10, where it sets the gain of the common-emitter amplifier.

Analogy — power steering and pumps

An emitter follower is power steering. Your hands on the wheel (the input) do almost no work — they just indicate a position. The hydraulic booster (the transistor, fed from the collector rail) does the actual shoving, and the road wheels (the load) get full force. Crucially, the position faithfully follows the wheel: gain one, muscle many. The 0.6 V drop is the little bit of slack in the linkage — a fixed dead travel you learn to expect, and the follower's version of it is at least constant enough to design around.

For the second half of the module: a voltage source is a pressurized tank — fixed pressure, and it will supply any flow the plumbing demands. A current source is a positive-displacement pump — fixed litres per minute, regardless of back-pressure. Narrow the pipe (raise the load resistance) and the pump just develops more pressure to keep the same flow moving. Where the analogy fails, it fails instructively: a real pump stalls when the back-pressure exceeds what its motor can develop. A real current source does exactly the same — run out of supply-rail "pressure" and the current collapses. That stall point is called the compliance limit, and we'll measure it below.

Biasing a follower for AC signals

The follower above assumed the input signal already sits at a friendly DC level. Real signals — audio, sensor wiggles — are often centered on zero, and a follower running from a single positive supply can't follow anything below ground (the transistor would just turn off). The fix is the standard single-supply ritual, and every piece of it is an old friend:

  • Set the emitter near mid-supply with DC bias, so the output can swing equally far up and down before hitting a rail. Emitter at mid-supply means the base sits at mid-supply + 0.6 V.
  • Make that base voltage with a divider — Module 02's circuit, doing its most common job. Stiff enough that the base current doesn't sag it: the 10× rule, applied against the follower's own Zin ≈ βRE, wants RTh of the divider ≤ βRE/10.
  • Couple the signal in and out through capacitors — Modules 03 and 05: a capacitor passes the wiggle and blocks the DC level, forming a high-pass with whatever impedance it faces. Size each cap so the corner frequency 1/2πRC sits well below the lowest frequency you care about.

Worked design — a 12 V audio buffer. Choose a healthy bias current, IE ≈ 5 mA (re ≈ 5 Ω, nicely stiff). Emitter at mid-supply, 6 V, so RE = 6 V / 5 mA = 1.2 kΩ. Base needs 6.6 V: the divider ratio is 6.6/12 = 0.55, and R1 = 10 kΩ over R2 = 12 kΩ gives 12 × 12/22 = 6.55 V — close enough (VE ≈ 5.95 V, IE ≈ 5.0 mA). Check the stiffness: divider RTh = 10k∥12k ≈ 5.5 kΩ, versus βRE ≈ 120 kΩ looking into the base — better than 10× with room to spare. Input cap: the signal sees the divider RTh in parallel with the base's Zin, about 5 kΩ; 1 µF gives a corner near 30 Hz. This exact bias point — 5 mA into 1.2 kΩ — is the one running in the widget above.

Limits — the follower only pushes

Look at the circuit again and notice the asymmetry. On upward swings the transistor pulls the output up actively — through the collector, from the rail, with as much current as β allows. On downward swings the transistor can't help at all: a transistor only conducts one way, and the most it can do is back off toward zero current. Everything that pulls the output down is plain old RE, a passive resistor doing its best.

Into a light load, no one notices. Into a heavy or capacitive load, the down-swing is limited to the current RE can sink — and if the signal dives faster than RE can drag the load down, the transistor simply cuts off: VBE collapses below 0.6 V, the emitter stops following, and the output waveform's bottom half gets sliced off while RE and the load lazily discharge on their own schedule. You can throw current at the problem (smaller RE, more standing power), but the elegant fix is a second, upside-down (PNP) follower to do the pulling down — a push–pull output stage, which is exactly where Module 11 picks up the story.

Current sources — the recipe

Here's the beautiful part: you already know this circuit. Take the biased follower and shift your attention from the emitter to the collector. Fix the base voltage VB — with a divider, a Zener, a reference, anything stiff. The emitter obediently sits at VB − 0.6, so the current through RE is nailed down:

I = (VB − 0.6 V) / RE and collector current ≈ emitter current — set by VB and RE, not by the load

Now the punchline. That current has nowhere to come from but the collector (base current is a β-fold smaller correction). So whatever sits in the collector path — an LED string, a resistor, an amplifier stage — gets that current forced through it. The load doesn't set the current; the emitter side already did. Change the load and the collector voltage moves to whatever value pushes the same current — the positive-displacement pump, developing however much pressure the pipe demands.

Compliance — check it before celebrating. The transistor needs VCE above about 0.2 V to keep transistor-ing; push it lower and it saturates (Module 08) and becomes a sad closed switch. So the collector may sit anywhere from about 0.2 V above the emitter up to near the positive rail — that window is the compliance range, the span of load voltage over which the "fixed litres per minute" promise holds. Demand a load drop bigger than the window and the current collapses to whatever Ohm's law allows with the transistor fully on.

Why bother? Because so many things are current-shaped problems: LED strings — brightness is set by current, and an LED's steep V–I curve makes voltage drive a thermal gamble (the design bench quantifies it); biasing — a current source holds an amplifier's operating point steady against supply and temperature wobble; active loads — replace a collector resistor with a current source and an amplifier's gain multiplies enormously (a Module-10 punchline); ramp generators — constant current into a capacitor gives a perfectly straight voltage ramp, the heart of every classic oscilloscope sweep.

H&H rules of thumb
  • A follower drops 0.6 V, always. Budget for it; don't be surprised by it.
  • Impedance transformation: Zin ≈ β·Zload looking into the base, Zout ≈ Zsource/β looking into the emitter — plus the little re.
  • re = 25 mV / IC: 25 Ω at 1 mA. It's the floor under every follower's output impedance; more bias current buys a stiffer output.
  • A follower pulls up through the transistor but only pulls down through RE — expect asymmetric drive into heavy loads.
  • A current source's current is set by VB and RE, NOT by the load: I = (VB − 0.6)/RE.
  • Check compliance before celebrating: the load may drop from ~0 up to (Vsupply − VE − 0.2 V). Past that, the transistor saturates and the "constant" current quietly becomes Ohm's law.

The sensor from Module 02: a signal source with 100 kΩ output impedance, which must drive a 1 kΩ load. Wired directly it delivers about 1% of its signal. Design a single-supply (12 V) AC-coupled follower to fix it — bias point, all resistor values, coupling — and estimate the delivered fraction. How close to 95%+ can you get, and what does it take?

Show solution

Direct: 1k/(100k + 1k) ≈ 0.99%. Garbage, as before.

First attempt, one transistor (β = 100): bias as in the worked design — emitter at 6 V, RE = 1.2 kΩ (≈5 mA, re ≈ 5 Ω), coupling caps in and out. The AC emitter load is RE∥RL ≈ 545 Ω, so the delivered fraction is roughly 545/(545 + 5 + 100k/101) ≈ 35%. A 35-fold improvement — but capped well short of 95%, because 100 kΩ/β ≈ 1 kΩ of transformed source impedance is still comparable to the load. One β of 100 isn't enough transformation for this job.

Real fix: two followers in cascade — a Darlington, β ≈ 100 × 100 = 10⁴ (it drops 2 × 0.6 = 1.2 V; budget it). Emitter at ~5.5 V, RE = 1 kΩ (5.5 mA, re ≈ 4.5 Ω). Base needs 5.5 + 1.2 = 6.7 V: R1 = 1 MΩ over R2 = 1.3 MΩ gives 6.78 V (base current is only 5.5 mA/10⁴ ≈ 0.55 µA, sagging the 565 kΩ divider by ~0.3 V — acceptable, or trim R2). Now Zin at the base is 10⁴ × (1k∥1k) ≈ 5 MΩ… but the divider RTh of 565 kΩ sits in parallel, so the source sees ≈ 508 kΩ and the total delivered fraction is ≈ 81%. The transistor is no longer the bottleneck — the bias network is.

The last 15%: bootstrap the bias (AoE §2.4.3): feed the divider tap to the base through a series resistor and tie a capacitor from the emitter back to that tap. Since the emitter follows the base, the AC voltage across the series resistor is nearly zero, so almost no signal current flows into the bias network — its effective AC impedance soars, the source sees the full ~5 MΩ, and the delivered fraction lands at ≈ 95%. From 1% to 95%: that is what "impedance transformer" means.

From a 15 V supply, design a 10 mA current source whose load (returned to the 15 V rail) may drop anywhere from 0 to at least 10 V. Choose VB, the divider, and RE; then verify the compliance arithmetic explicitly.

Show solution

Keep VE low — every volt on the emitter is a volt stolen from compliance. But not too low: VE around 1–2 V keeps the current insensitive to VBE's ±tens-of-mV wobble. Choose VB = 2.6 V, so VE = 2.0 V and RE = 2.0 V / 10 mA = 200 Ω (an E24 value; drops 20 mW — any ¼ W part).

Divider: base current is 10 mA/β ≈ 100 µA, so run ≥ 10× that through the divider — 2 mA, i.e. about 7.5 kΩ total. R1 = 6.2 kΩ, R2 = 1.3 kΩ (both E24): 15 × 1.3/7.5 = 2.60 V exactly. (Pedant's check: 100 µA through RTh = 6.2k∥1.3k ≈ 1.1 kΩ sags the base ~0.11 V, landing near 9.5 mA — trim RE to 180 Ω or stiffen the divider if the last 5% matters.)

Compliance, explicitly: the collector must stay above VE + 0.2 = 2.2 V, so the load may drop up to 15 − 2.2 = 12.8 V. Requirement was 10 V: met, with 2.8 V of margin. Worst-case transistor dissipation is with the load shorted: VCE = 13 V at 10 mA = 130 mW — fine for a small transistor, but the habit of checking is the point.

An LED backlight string: three red LEDs (Vf ≈ 2.0 V each) at 20 mA, from a nominally 12 V supply that actually wobbles between 10 and 14 V. Design the drive twice — (a) a plain series resistor, (b) a transistor current source — and compute the brightness (current) variation both ways across the supply range.

Show solution

(a) Series resistor. The string drops 3 × 2.0 = 6 V. At nominal 12 V the resistor sees 6 V, so R = 6 V / 20 mA = 300 Ω. Now wobble the supply: at 10 V, I = (10−6)/300 = 13.3 mA; at 14 V, I = (14−6)/300 = 26.7 mA. A 2:1 brightness swing — ±33% around nominal — visible to any eyeball, and it rides every supply dip. The problem: the resistor only gets the leftover voltage, and the leftover is where all the wobble lives.

(b) Current source. String in the collector path from the supply rail. Hold VB fixed at 2.7 V — and here "fixed" must mean referenced to something stable (a Zener, a reference, a regulated rail), not a divider from the wobbling supply, or VB wobbles proportionally and you're back to ±21%. Then VE = 2.1 V and RE = 2.1 V / 20 mA = 105 Ω (100 Ω gives 21 mA — fine). Compliance at the worst-case 10 V supply: need 6 (string) + 2.1 (VE) + 0.2 (VCE,sat) = 8.3 V — and we have 10 V, so the source stays in compliance with 1.7 V spare. Current variation across 10–14 V: essentially zero — the extra supply volts just land on VCE, where nobody cares. That's the whole argument for current-driving LEDs in one design.
