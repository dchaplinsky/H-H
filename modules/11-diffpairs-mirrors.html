Differential Pairs, Mirrors & Push-Pull

Three two-transistor tricks — steering a current, copying a current, and sharing a load between an NPN and a PNP — that turn out to be the entire skeleton of the op-amp. AoE 3rd ed. §2.3–2.4.

Why this matters

Crack open any op-amp — the venerable 741, a modern rail-to-rail part, it doesn't matter — and you find the same floor plan: a differential pair at the front door, current mirrors serving as loads and passing bias around the chip, and a push-pull stage muscling the output. One transistor was an amplifier; two transistors, matched and cooperating, are a building block. This module is the parts list for the next five modules' hero: when we treat the op-amp as a magic triangle, everything inside the triangle is something you are about to build by hand. Learn these three circuits and datasheet phrases like "input offset," "CMRR," and "class-AB output" stop being vocabulary and start being floor plan.

Physics background

Why "differential" at all? Because the world is noisy in common mode. Run two wires from a sensor across a room and both pick up the same 50/60 Hz hum, the same thermocouple drifts, the same ground bounce — the garbage arrives equally on both wires. A circuit that answers only to the difference between its two inputs is deaf to all of it. That's the differential amplifier's contract: amplify V₁ − V₂, ignore (V₁ + V₂)/2.

Why matched pairs? Module 08 taught that VBE moves −2 mV/°C — a single transistor's operating point wanders with every draft. But put two transistors side by side on one silicon die and they are the same age, the same doping, the same temperature, forever. Their drifts are a common-mode signal — and a differential circuit cancels those too. Matching doesn't remove the transistor's flaws; it arranges for the flaws to arrive equally on both sides, where the circuit subtracts them away. This one idea is why monolithic ICs beat hand-picked discrete parts.

The long-tailed pair — steering a current

Take two matched NPNs and tie their emitters together. Feed that shared node from one of Module 09's current sources — the "long tail" — sinking a fixed Itail. The two collectors go through equal resistors RC to the positive supply, and the two bases are the inputs.

The tail current has nowhere to go except through the two transistors, so IC1 + IC2 = Itail, always. With equal base voltages the pair is symmetric and the tail splits 50/50. Now nudge one base up by a few millivolts: that transistor's VBE grows, its twin's shrinks, and — because collector current is exponential in VBE (Ebers–Moll, Module 08) — current steers from one side to the other. Nothing is created or destroyed; the input merely decides how the fixed total is shared. Work out the two exponentials and their ratio collapses into one clean function of the input difference ΔV:

IC1 − IC2 = Itail · tanh(ΔV / 2VT) VT = 25 mV; each side individually is Itail/2 · (1 ± tanh(ΔV/2VT))

The tanh is the pair's signature. Near zero it is a straight line — for |ΔV| ≲ 25 mV the steering is nicely linear (the curve departs from the straight line by only ~8% at ±25 mV). Beyond that it bends over hard: at ΔV = 60 mV one transistor already hogs 92% of the tail, and by ±100 mV the steering is essentially complete (98%) — the pair has become a switch, a fact ECL logic exploits on purpose. The slope in the middle is the small-signal gain: at balance each transistor runs at Itail/2, so each has gm = (Itail/2)/VT, and the differential output current per volt of ΔV is exactly that gm. The tail current sets everything — gain, input range in current terms, power burned. Want more gain? More tail. It's one knob.

Differential vs common-mode — and why the tail must be a source

Feed the two inputs a pure difference (one up, one down) and current steers: the collector voltages swing apart. Differential gain, taking the output between the two collectors:

Gdiff = gm·RC = RC / 2re output taken differentially, collector to collector; a single collector gives half of this

Now raise both inputs together — a common-mode signal, the hum on both wires. Both VBEs try to grow, the shared emitter node simply rises by the same amount, and — if the tail really is a current source — the total current cannot change, so the split doesn't change and the collectors don't move. Common-mode gain ≈ 0. The ratio of wanted gain to unwanted gain is the common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR), and here is the design punchline: a good tail source = high CMRR. If you cheat and use a plain resistor Rtail instead, a common-mode wiggle changes the voltage across it, hence the total current, hence both collector voltages: GCM ≈ RC/2Rtail. A resistor big enough to fix that would starve the pair of bias current. A transistor current source gives you the DC current of a small resistor with the incremental stiffness of a multi-megohm one — Module 09's circuit, doing exactly the job it was born for.

Current mirrors — copying a current

Second trick, same ingredient: matching. Take a transistor and short its collector to its base ("diode-connected"). Push a current Iprog into it — say through a resistor from the supply — and the transistor obligingly develops exactly the VBE that makes it carry that current. Now wire that VBE across the base–emitter of a matched twin. Same VBE, same die, same temperature, same Ebers–Moll curve → same collector current. The output transistor mirrors the programmed current, whatever its collector voltage happens to be doing — which is precisely the definition of a current source.

Iout ≈ Iprog = (Vsupply − 0.6 V) / Rprog one resistor programs the master; every slave copies it — the ratio trick behind IC biasing

Why chips adore mirrors: bias distribution — one precision resistor programs one master, and dozens of slaves scattered across the die each get a clean copy, no resistor forest needed. And the star turn: as an active load for the differential pair. Replace the two collector resistors with a mirror and the mirror takes Q1's signal current, flips it, and delivers it to Q2's collector — the two half-signals that used to appear on separate collectors now add on a single node, doubling the gain into a single-ended output (recovering the factor of two we lost taking one collector), and the "resistor" it presents is a transistor's collector: hundreds of kilohms of incremental resistance without the DC drop of a real one. Diff pair plus mirror load is, almost verbatim, the front half of every op-amp schematic you'll ever see.

The fine print, in one sentence: the copy isn't perfect, because the output transistor's current creeps up a few percent as its collector voltage rises — the Early effect — which is why precision mirrors add emitter resistors or extra transistors (Wilson, cascode) to stiffen the copy. And the whole scheme lives and dies on matching: two loose 2N3904s from the drawer differ by tens of millivolts of VBE — enough for a 2:1 current error — and drift apart with every degree of temperature difference. Mirrors belong on one die.

Push-pull — sharing the load

Module 09's emitter follower had an embarrassing asymmetry: its transistor could pull up with muscle, but pulling down was delegated to the emitter resistor — a lazy, current-limited pull that either wastes power (small RE) or chokes on fast negative swings (big RE). The fix is to hire a second employee for the other direction: an NPN follower on top to source current on positive swings, a PNP follower below to sink it on negative swings — push-pull. Each transistor idles at (nearly) zero current and works only its own half-cycle, so a class-B stage delivers watts to a load while dissipating little at rest.

The catch sits at the handoff. With both bases tied straight to the input, neither transistor conducts until the input clears a VBE: the NPN needs Vin > +0.6 V, the PNP needs Vin < −0.6 V. Every zero crossing drags the signal through a ±0.6 V dead zone where the output simply flatlines — crossover distortion. It's worst exactly where you can't ignore it: at low volume, where the dead zone is a large fraction of the whole signal.

The fix is to bias both transistors slightly on: insert two diode drops between the two bases, so the pair idles with roughly a VBE apiece already applied and the handoff happens with both hands on the rope — class AB. Two housekeeping notes from the real world: the idle current is thermally treacherous (a hot output transistor's VBE falls, so it idles harder, gets hotter… — thermal runaway), so the bias diodes are mounted to share the output transistors' heatsink, and small emitter ballast resistors (tenths of an ohm) are added so a little extra current costs a little extra voltage, stabilizing the idle point.

H&H rules of thumb
  • A diff pair without emitter degeneration is linear over roughly ±25 mV. More swing than that and the tanh bends; by ±100 mV it's a switch. Emitter resistors trade gain for a wider linear window, exactly as in Module 10.
  • The tail current sets everything. Each side idles at Itail/2, so each side's gm = (Itail/2)/25 mV — gain, speed, and power all hang off one number.
  • Mirror accuracy lives and dies on matching — same die, same temperature, or don't bother. A few millivolts of VBE mismatch is tens of percent of current error.
  • Push-pull without bias = audibly gritty crossover at low volume. The dead zone is a fixed ±0.6 V, so the quieter the music, the larger the fraction it eats.
  • Two diodes + small emitter ballast resistors = the standard fix. Diodes pre-bias the pair into class AB; the ballasts (and heatsinking the diodes with the outputs) keep the idle current from running away thermally.
Balance-scale analogy
  • The diff pair is a balance scale — a seesaw with two pans sharing a fixed total of water (the tail current). Tilt it (ΔV) and water pours from one pan into the other, but the total never changes: a small tilt moves water in proportion (the linear region), a big tilt dumps everything into one pan (the tanh saturating). Raise both pans together — common mode — and the balance doesn't care: no tilt, no transfer.
  • The mirror is a master/slave hydraulic pair: push fluid through the master cylinder and the slave, plumbed to the same pressure, delivers the identical flow at its end — one flow programmed, a faithful copy delivered somewhere more useful.
  • Push-pull is two people hauling a rope hand-over-hand — one pulls it up, the other pulls it down, each resting during the other's stroke. Crossover distortion is the fumble at the handoff: for an instant neither has a grip, and the rope goes slack.

Where each fails: a real balance scale has friction and arm-length mismatch — and so does the pair (VBE offset appears as a false tilt: input offset voltage). The hydraulic slave leaks a little more as its outlet pressure rises — that's the Early effect. And the rope team's handoff can be over-fixed: grip too hard simultaneously (too much idle bias) and they fight each other and overheat — thermal runaway.

Check yourself

Design bench

Pencil and paper, plus Module 09's current-source recipe and the 25 mV reflex. Check the box when you've done it and verified against the solution.

Single 12 V supply. Build a differential pair with a 2 mA tail using Module 09's current-source recipe (pick VB and RE), then choose RC so each collector sits about 3 V below the supply at balance. Finally, compute the differential gain.

Show solution

The tail. Module 09's recipe: hold the base at VB, and the emitter resistor programs I = (VB − 0.6)/RE. Pick VB = 1.6 V (a stiff divider from 12 V), putting the emitter at 1.0 V; then RE = 1.0 V / 2 mA = 500 Ω → 510 Ω standard (1.96 mA — close enough).

The collectors. At balance each side carries 1 mA. For a ~3 V drop: RC = 3 V / 1 mA = 3 kΩ; take the standard 2.7 kΩ and verify: 1 mA × 2.7 k = 2.7 V drop, collectors resting at 9.3 V ✓ — plenty of headroom for swings both ways.

The gain. Each side idles at 1 mA, so gm = 1 mA / 25 mV = 40 mS (re = 25 Ω). Taken differentially between the collectors, G = gmRC = 40 mS × 2.7 k ≈ 108; off a single collector you get half, ≈ 54 — which is exactly the factor of two a mirror active load wins back. Sanity check against Module 10's habit: RC/2re = 2700/50 = 54 per collector ✓.

From a 12 V supply, program a PNP current mirror to deliver 1 mA: choose the programming resistor (then the nearest standard value). Then the physics question: if the output transistor runs hotter than the diode-connected master, what happens to the output current, and roughly how fast?

Show solution

Programming resistor. The master eats one VBE, the resistor gets the rest: R = (12 − 0.6) V / 1 mA = 11.4 kΩ → 11 kΩ standard, giving 1.04 mA. Every matched slave now copies ~1 mA anywhere on the die.

Heat. VBE falls ~2 mV/°C at constant current — so a hotter slave, fed the master's unchanged VBE, carries more current: the copy rises by e2mV/25mV ≈ ×1.08 per °C — about +8% per degree, and a factor of two for only ~9 °C of mismatch. That's why "matched" means thermally coupled, not just electrically similar: on one die the pair drifts together and the error cancels; across two discrete packages it doesn't.

±12 V rails, push-pull follower, 8 Ω speaker, 1 W of clean sine. Find the required peak output voltage and peak current. Then explain why a plain class-B version of this stage sounds fine loud but buzzes at whisper volume, and specify the standard fix.

Show solution

Peaks. P = Vrms²/R, and Vpk = √2·Vrms, so Vpk = √(2PR) = √(2·1·8) = 4 V and Ipk = 4 V/8 Ω = 0.5 A. ±12 V rails have room to spare (a follower loses no gain, just a VBE of headroom per side) — but note 0.5 A is real transistor territory, not a small-signal part.

The whisper problem. The class-B dead zone is a fixed ±0.6 V. At full 1 W (4 V peak) it's 15% of the amplitude and the ear mostly forgives it; turn down to a 0.7 V-peak whisper and the dead zone is nearly the whole signal — the output spends most of each cycle silent, and what survives is gritty harmonic hash. Crossover distortion is the rare defect that gets worse as you turn it down.

The fix. Class AB: two diodes between the bases pre-bias both transistors just into conduction, plus ~0.5 Ω emitter ballast resistors and the diodes thermally mounted with the output pair, so the idle current stays a few tens of milliamps instead of running away as the stage warms up.
