Active Filters

One op-amp, two resistors and two capacitors buy you what no RC ladder can: a filter with a genuinely sharp corner — and a new personality knob called Q that decides whether it settles politely or rings like a struck bell. AoE 3rd ed. Ch. 6.

Why this matters

Module 05's RC filter is a lovely thing, but it rolls off at a polite 6 dB/octave and it sags near the corner — at f3dB the signal you meant to keep is already down 30%. Need a steeper skirt? Your first instinct — stack several RC sections in a row — runs straight into Module 02: each stage loads the one before it, dragging corners around and blunting the very knee you were trying to sharpen. An op-amp fixes both problems at once. Its buffering makes cascaded stages truly independent, so their dB simply add; and — the deeper trick — a little positive feedback lets two capacitors conspire to produce a response no passive RC network can make at all: flat right up to the corner, then down like a cliff. No inductors, no loading, stages that snap together like bricks. This is how every anti-aliasing filter in front of an ADC and every serious audio filter is actually built.

Physics background

Why second order changes the game. One energy-storage element (one capacitor) gives a first-order response: a single real pole, a gentle exponential personality, 6 dB/octave, no drama possible. Add a second storage element and energy can now slosh back and forth between the two — the circuit acquires a natural frequency and, with it, the possibility of resonance. Mathematically the two poles can leave the real axis and become a complex pair; physically, the circuit can overshoot and oscillate on its way to equilibrium, like a mass bouncing on a spring.

Q is the knob that sets how much slosh. Low Q means heavy damping: energy transferred between the caps is mostly lost each cycle and the response droops early. High Q means the exchange is efficient: the filter peaks near the corner — gain above unity from a passive-looking network! — before falling. Tuned just right (Q = 0.707), the peak exactly cancels the droop and the passband comes out maximally flat.

The op-amp's role is subtle and beautiful. Two RC sections in a passive ladder always give two boring real poles — the resistors damp everything and the response is mushier than even a single RC near its corner. The op-amp bootstraps one capacitor: instead of tying it to ground, the amplifier drives its far end with a copy of the output, so the cap feeds energy back into the network at just the right phase to imitate an inductor's resonance. That positive feedback is what pushes the poles off the real axis and makes them complex. Its second job is mundane but essential: the low-impedance output buffers each stage from the next, so sections cascade without loading (Module 02's ten-to-one rule becomes irrelevant — the op-amp gives you ten-thousand-to-one for free).

Reviewing the slope zoo

Vocabulary first. A filter's order is the number of poles — loosely, the number of energy-storage elements doing real work. Each pole contributes 6 dB/octave (20 dB/decade) of ultimate rolloff:

ultimate slope = N × 6 dB/octave = N × 20 dB/decade N poles; a 4th-order filter falls 24 dB/octave far past the corner

So why not just chain four passive RCs and call it fourth order? You can — the ultimate slope really is 24 dB/octave — but the knee is a disaster. Each section's R loads the previous section's C (the divider isn't driving an open circuit anymore), the corners smear apart, and the region near f3dB becomes a long, droopy shoulder: at the nominal corner a chain of four identical RCs is down about 10 dB, not 3. Buffer between the sections, though, and each one sees an ideal source and an open-circuit load: the responses multiply exactly, the dB add exactly, and — with the resonance trick below — you can even shape each section's knee so the combination comes out flat. That's the whole active-filter recipe: buffered second-order sections, cascaded.

The Sallen-Key low-pass

The workhorse topology, and the gentlest introduction. Take two equal resistors in series, two equal capacitors, and an amplifier of gain K (an op-amp with a resistive gain network — or a bare wire for K = 1). The second capacitor goes to ground, as in any RC low-pass. The first capacitor is the trick: it does not go to ground — it goes to the output. Near the corner, the amplifier drives that capacitor's bottom plate up and down in sympathy with the signal, canceling part of the current that would otherwise leak away through it — positive feedback, strongest right at the corner, weaker above and below. Working the node equations (do it once in your life — two nodes, Module 02 algebra) gives, for the equal-component version:

fc = 1 / (2πRC)      Q = 1 / (3 − K) equal-component Sallen-Key: corner set by RC, Q set purely by the amplifier gain K

Read that Q formula like a story. K = 1 (unity-gain follower): Q = 0.5, two coincident real poles, the sluggish over-damped case. K = 1.586: Q = 0.707 — the magic Butterworth value, maximally flat. Push on: K = 2.5 gives Q = 2 and a visible resonant peak. And as K → 3 the denominator goes to zero, Q → ∞: the positive feedback fully replaces the losses and the circuit stops being a filter and becomes an oscillator. A filter and an oscillator are the same circuit with the damping knob at different settings — one of electronics' great punchlines, and Chapter 7's opening act.

The response families

Once you can place complex poles anywhere, where should you put them? There is no single best answer — only trade-offs, and three classic compromises with names you'll see on every filter datasheet:

Butterworth (maximally flat): poles placed so the passband is as flat as mathematics allows — every derivative of the response that can be zeroed at DC, is. For a 2nd-order section that means Q = 0.707. No ripple, a clean −3 dB at the corner, a decent knee. The default choice when you care about amplitude accuracy across the passband — which is to say, the default choice.

Bessel (maximally flat delay): poles placed to keep the time delay equal at all passband frequencies, so a waveform's components arrive together and its shape survives. Q ≈ 0.58 for 2nd order. The frequency-domain knee is noticeably softer than Butterworth's — that's the price — but a step passes through with barely 0.5% overshoot. Choose it for pulses, square waves, and anything a scope will look at.

Chebyshev (sharpest knee, with ripple): give up passband flatness — allow the response to ripple up and down by a specified amount (0.5 dB, 1 dB…) — and in exchange the response dives past the corner dramatically faster. Section Qs run higher than Butterworth's, so it also rings harder in the time domain. Choose it when the stopband spec is brutal and the passband can tolerate some waviness.

One family, one priority: Butterworth flattest passband, Bessel best waveforms, Chebyshev steepest skirt. You cannot have all three — pole placement is a budget, and each family spends it differently. For any 2nd-order low-pass, the whole magnitude story is one formula:

|H(f)| = 1 / √( (1 − x²)² + x²/Q² )    with   x = f/fc 2nd-order low-pass magnitude; sanity check: at x = 1 it collapses to |H| = Q exactly

Check the landmarks: at x ≪ 1, |H| → 1. At x ≫ 1, |H| → 1/x² — there's the 12 dB/octave. And at x = 1 exactly, |H| = Q: a Q = 3 filter is +9.5 dB up at its own corner, while Butterworth's Q = 0.707 lands precisely on −3 dB. The corner gain is the Q — worth tattooing somewhere.

Cascading to higher order

Need more than 12 dB/octave? Don't crank Q — cascade. An N-th-order filter factors into N/2 second-order sections (plus one first-order section if N is odd), each buffered by its own op-amp so nothing loads anything. The crucial subtlety: the sections are not identical. A 4th-order Butterworth is not two 2nd-order Butterworths in a row — that would droop −6 dB at the corner. Instead the standard tables prescribe one low-Q section and one peaking section whose bump exactly fills the other's sag:

4th-order Butterworth = section at Q = 0.541 × section at Q = 1.307 same fc for both; the product is exactly 1/√(1 + x⁸) — flat, then 24 dB/octave

(Those Qs are just geometry: Butterworth poles sit on a circle at 22.5° and 67.5° from the axis, and Q = 1/(2 cos θ) gives 0.541 and 1.307.) Because each section is buffered, you build them independently, verify them independently, and multiply — the frequency domain's reward for the op-amp's isolation. Every filter handbook tabulates section Qs (and, for Chebyshev, per-section corner shifts) for any order and family; your job is only to pick the row and turn each line of the table into one Sallen-Key stage.

High-pass and band-pass

High-pass: swap every R for a C and every C for an R. That's the entire recipe. The series elements become capacitors (blocking DC, as a high-pass must), the bootstrap element becomes a resistor, and the same formulas hold with the response mirrored: fc = 1/(2πRC), Q = 1/(3 − K) for the equal-component version, magnitude |H| = x²/√((1−x²)² + x²/Q²). Module 05's "where's the cap?" reflex still works: caps in series with the signal path — it's a high-pass.

Band-pass: the pedestrian route is a high-pass cascaded with a low-pass — perfectly respectable when the band is wide (corners a decade apart, say). For narrow bands you want a single resonant section with its center frequency and Q set independently; the clean way is the state-variable filter (AoE §6.3.3) — three op-amps wired as two integrators in a loop, which hands you low-pass, high-pass, and band-pass outputs simultaneously, with Q adjustable by a single resistor and far less component sensitivity than a high-Q Sallen-Key. When a design needs Q above ~3, reach for it.

H&H rules of thumb
  • 6 dB/octave per pole — count poles, know the ultimate slope. The knee is the family's business; the slope is the order's.
  • Butterworth for frequency-domain flatness, Bessel for waveform fidelity, Chebyshev when the knee matters more than ripple. Pick the priority first, the family follows.
  • Q > 0.707 peaks before it rolls — in the frequency domain a bump at the corner, in the time domain overshoot and ringing. The two are the same fact.
  • The op-amp must be boringly ideal at your corner: gain-bandwidth product at least ~20 × fc·Q, or the amp's own phase lag shifts your poles and your carefully chosen Q isn't what you built (Module 18 makes this quantitative).
  • 1% resistors and NP0/C0G or film capacitors. The corner is only as accurate as RC, and Q is only as stable as the gain network; ±10% ceramics that drift with temperature and voltage make "precision filter" a contradiction.
Car-suspension analogy

A car's suspension — springs plus the car's mass — is a genuine 2nd-order system, and Q is the shock absorbers:

  • Worn shocks = high Q. Push down on the fender and the car bounces several times: a resonant peak. On the road, any bump near the resonant frequency gets amplified — the boat-like wallow of a car overdue for new dampers.
  • Overdamped = low Q (0.5 and below). Rock-hard shocks: no bounce ever, but the body takes ages to return to level and the ride is sluggish and sloshy. Filters do the same — a Q = 0.5 section creeps to its final value.
  • Critically damped-ish ≈ Butterworth "just right". One barely-there overshoot, quick settling, comfortable — the tuning most passenger cars aim near.
  • A pothole is a step input. Bessel is the limousine tuning: the shape of the road insult is absorbed with almost no aftermath. Chebyshev is the track car: brutally flat cornering (a razor knee) at the cost of rattling your teeth on every expansion joint (ripple and ringing).

Where the analogy fails: a suspension is one mechanical degree of freedom with one damping knob, but filter families differ in pole placement — Bessel vs Chebyshev is not just "more or less damping," it's a different geometric arrangement of poles, section by section. And nothing mechanical mirrors the op-amp's bootstrapping trick: no spring in your car feeds a scaled copy of the body's motion back under the wheels. (Active suspension comes close — and is, fittingly, also built around amplifiers.)

Check yourself

Design bench

Pencil, paper, and the corner formula. The filter tables can wait — everything here is 2nd order (with one optional upgrade). Check the box when you've verified against the solution.

An ADC samples at 100 kHz; your signal band is DC–10 kHz. Anything above the 50 kHz Nyquist frequency will alias irreversibly into the band, so it must be attenuated before sampling. Design a 2nd-order Butterworth Sallen-Key low-pass with fc = 15 kHz (comfortably above the signal, comfortably below Nyquist). Use the equal-component topology with C = 1 nF: find R, pick the standard value, then compute the actual attenuation at 50 kHz and decide honestly whether one section is enough.

Show solution

Values. R = 1/(2πfcC) = 1/(2π × 15 kHz × 1 nF) ≈ 10.6 kΩ. Nearest common value 10 kΩ moves the corner to fc = 1/(2π × 10 kΩ × 1 nF) ≈ 15.9 kHz — the corner moved up, which costs stopband, so check the numbers again rather than assuming. Butterworth Q = 0.707 needs K = 1.586: Rg = 10 kΩ, Rf = 5.9 kΩ (K = 1.59, Q = 0.709 — close enough).

At 50 kHz: x = 50/15.9 = 3.14. Far past the corner |H| ≈ 1/x² = 0.101; the exact formula gives 1/√((1−9.87)² + 9.87/0.5) = 0.101 too — −19.9 dB. Passband check: at 10 kHz, x = 0.63, |H| = 0.93 → −0.63 dB of droop. Acceptable for most sensors; calibrate it out if it isn't.

Is −20 dB enough? That's ×0.10 — an aliased interferer is knocked down only tenfold. Fine if the spectrum above 50 kHz is already quiet; feeble for a 12-bit ADC in a noisy environment. The upgrade costs one more op-amp: a 4th-order Butterworth — two cascaded sections at the same corner with Q = 0.541 and Q = 1.307 — reaches −39.8 dB at 50 kHz (×0.01) with the 10 kHz droop improving to −0.10 dB. That double win — steeper skirt and flatter passband — is exactly why cascaded active sections beat any amount of passive cleverness.

Module 05's Task 1 built a 20 Hz high-pass rumble filter from one capacitor and the next stage's 47 kΩ input — and admitted its 6 dB/octave skirt was "lazy." Upgrade it: a 2nd-order Butterworth Sallen-Key high-pass at fc = 20 Hz. Describe the topology swap, pick equal-component values using C = 470 nF, and compare rejection at 5 Hz (deep rumble) against the old single RC.

Show solution

Topology. Take the Sallen-Key low-pass and swap every R for a C and every C for an R: two 470 nF capacitors now sit in series with the signal (blocking DC — a high-pass must), the bootstrap element becomes a resistor to the output, and the shunt element a resistor to ground. Same formulas, mirrored response.

Values. R = 1/(2πfcC) = 1/(2π × 20 × 470 nF) ≈ 16.9 kΩ — an E96 value that exists, giving fc = 20.0 Hz on the nose. Gain network as before: Rg = 10 kΩ, Rf = 5.9 kΩ for K = 1.59, Q ≈ 0.71. Note the passband now has gain 1.59 (+4 dB) — budget for it downstream, or trim it out; the unity-gain variant with a 2:1 resistor ratio avoids it at the cost of unequal values. Use film capacitors: 470 nF of ordinary high-K ceramic would drift the corner audibly.

At 5 Hz (x = 0.25): the old single RC gives x/√(1+x²) = 0.243 → −12.3 dB. The 2nd-order Butterworth gives x²/√((1−x²)² + 2x²) = 0.0623 → −24.1 dB — four times less cone-flapping rumble voltage, while 20 Hz program material sits at the same −3 dB as before and 40 Hz is now flatter than the old filter left it (the Butterworth knee is squarer on both sides). Steeper skirt, better passband: the active upgrade in one line.

A colleague built an equal-component Sallen-Key designed for Q = 2 (a deliberately peaky response, K = 2.5 from a gain network of 5% resistors). It works — but every transient makes the output ring for many cycles, far worse than the 44% overshoot the design predicted. Boards vary: some ring viciously, a few are nearly fine. What's going on, and what are the fixes?

Show solution

The culprit is Q = 1/(3 − K) itself. Differentiate it: dQ/dK = Q². Sensitivity grows as the square of Q — at Q = 2, a 1% shift in K moves Q by 4%; near K = 3 the formula is a cliff edge. With 5% resistors setting K = 1 + Rf/Rg, the ratio can land 10% high in the worst case: K = 2.66, and Q = 1/(3 − 2.66) ≈ 2.9 — overshoot climbs from 44% to ~59% and the ring-down time stretches in proportion to Q. Board-to-board spread in two cheap resistors explains the lottery. (Had the drift pushed K past 3, the boards would oscillate outright — same cliff, further along.)

Fixes, in order of preference. (1) If the application doesn't truly need Q = 2, redesign at Q ≤ 0.707 using the unity-gain topology: K = 1 is set by a piece of wire — no resistors, no drift, Q determined by a capacitor ratio instead (far gentler sensitivity). (2) If the peaky Q is required, set K with 1% (or 0.1%) resistors: a ±2% ratio bounds Q between 1.9 and 2.1 — the design behaves. (3) For Q much above ~3, abandon Sallen-Key for a state-variable filter, whose Q sensitivity is benign by construction. The meta-lesson: always ask not just "what are the component values?" but "how hard does the circuit punish their tolerances?"
