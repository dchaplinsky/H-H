Op-Amp Golden Rules

A million-fold amplifier that is useless by itself — until negative feedback tames it into exactly the circuit you asked for. Two rules replace two chapters of transistor arithmetic. AoE 3rd ed. §4.1–4.2.

Why this matters

Modules 08–11 were an apprenticeship in transistor honesty: every follower cost you 0.6 V, every common-emitter stage drifted with temperature, every bias network was a negotiation. Then Module 11 showed the endgame — a differential pair at the front, current mirrors passing bias around, a push-pull stage at the output. Put those blocks on one die, crank the gain to something absurd, and sell it for pennies: that is an op-amp, the perfected transistor circuit. It opens Part III because from here on you mostly stop building amplifiers out of transistors and start building systems out of op-amps.

The magic ingredient is not the gain — it's what negative feedback does with it. Left open-loop, a gain of 10⁶ turns a 20 µV thermal wobble into a rail-slammed output: useless. Close the loop through two resistors and that same ferocious gain spends itself enforcing a simple contract, and the circuit's behavior is set by the resistors — parts you can buy to 0.1% — not by the transistors inside. Two "golden rules" capture the whole contract, and with them you can read or design op-amp circuits in your head, no Ebers–Moll required.

Physics background — what's inside the triangle

Every op-amp is Module 11 in a package: a differential pair at the inputs (often with a mirror as its active load), so the device responds to the difference V₊ − V₋ and rejects common-mode garbage; a high-gain voltage-gain stage in the middle; and a push-pull follower muscling the output. Multiply the stage gains and the open-loop gain AOL lands somewhere between 10⁵ and 10⁷ (100–140 dB) — deliberately huge, deliberately imprecise. The datasheet won't even pin it down; it varies part to part and with temperature, and the designers don't care. Why not?

Because the gain isn't meant to be used — it's meant to be spent. Feed a fraction of the output back to the inverting input and the amplifier now amplifies its own error: any difference between its two inputs gets multiplied by 10⁶ and thrown at the output with a sign that reduces that difference. The output slews until the error is essentially zero — with AOL = 2×10⁵, a 10 V output needs only 50 µV between the inputs. This is Horowitz & Hill's central idea, and the rest of the book is variations on it: an amplifier with enough gain, wrapped in negative feedback, becomes whatever the feedback network says it is. The bigger the raw gain, the more perfectly the closed-loop circuit obeys the resistors — which is why "absurdly huge and vague" is exactly the right specification.

The golden rules

H&H compress the entire ideal-op-amp theory into two statements. Everything in this module — and most of Part III — is these two rules plus Ohm's law.

The golden rules
  • I. The inputs draw no current.
  • II. With negative feedback, the output does whatever is necessary to make the voltage difference between the two inputs zero.

Rule I is a statement about the input stage: the diff pair's bases (or FET gates, in FET-input parts) sip nanoamps or picoamps — a million times less than the milliamps flowing in the surrounding resistors. Treat the inputs as perfect voltmeter probes: they observe a node without disturbing it. (The fine print — the nanoamps are not exactly zero and become the dominant error above roughly a megohm — is Module 18's problem.)

Rule II is the feedback contract, and it is the one people misquote. The op-amp does not "have" equal inputs the way a wire has equal ends — it drives its output, through whatever network you connected, until the inputs are forced equal. Read it honestly and the preconditions jump out: there must be a feedback path, the feedback must be negative (output coupled back to the − input), and the output must actually be able to do "whatever is necessary" — it can't swing past its supply rails or source unlimited current. Break any of these and rule II is off; the op-amp reverts to what it really is, a differential amplifier with gain 10⁶, and slams into a rail. We'll break the rules deliberately below.

The follower — feedback at its simplest

Wire the output straight back to the − input and feed the signal into +. Apply rule II: the output must make V₋ equal V₊ — and V₋ is the output. So Vout = Vin, exactly. That's the whole derivation.

Vout = Vin    (gain = 1) the op-amp follower: 100% feedback, the strongest contract the loop can enforce

Compare Module 09's emitter follower: that one dropped 0.6 V (and drifted −2 mV/°C), sourced current better than it sank it, and showed the load an output impedance of re — a few tens of ohms. The op-amp follower is the same idea perfected by feedback: no VBE offset (the loop cancels the output stage's drops — they're inside the loop, so the op-amp simply drives past them), input impedance in the gigohms (rule I), and an output impedance of the open-loop Zout divided by the loop gain — tens of ohms knocked down to milliohms. It's the ultimate buffer: Module 02's "10× rule" for connecting circuits stops being a compromise and becomes a formality.

The inverting amplifier

Now the classic. Ground the + input. Feed the signal through R₁ into the − input, and connect Rf from the − input to the output. Derive everything from the golden rules, step by step:

  1. Rule II: the + input sits at 0 V, so the output drives the − input to 0 V too. The − node sits at ground potential without being connected to ground.
  2. Ohm's law on R₁: the input side sees Vin across R₁ (one end at Vin, the other pinned at 0), so a current I = Vin/R₁ flows toward the − node.
  3. Rule I: none of that current can enter the op-amp. It has exactly one escape route: through Rf.
  4. Ohm's law on Rf: pushing I through Rf from a 0 V node means the far end must sit at Vout = 0 − I·Rf = −Vin·Rf/R₁.
G = −Rf / R₁     Zin = R₁ 10 k in, 100 k back → gain −10; the minus sign means the output mirrors the input upside-down

Concrete numbers, worth doing once by hand: R₁ = 10 k, Rf = 100 k, Vin = +1 V. Current in: 1 V / 10 k = 100 µA. That 100 µA continues through the 100 k, dropping 10 V — so the output sits at −10 V. Gain −10, and every volt of it is set by a resistor ratio.

The virtual ground — a place, not a wire

The − node deserves its own name: the virtual ground. It sits at 0 V, yet no wire ties it there — the op-amp's output, acting through Rf, holds it at zero by continuous active effort, the way a hovering drone "sits" at a fixed altitude. Two consequences. First, the input source only ever sees R₁ to a 0 V node, so Zin = R₁, period — that is the inverting amplifier's hidden cost. A gain of −100 with a 1 MΩ input impedance would need Rf = 100 MΩ; you can't have both. Second, the virtual ground is a current-summing point: several input resistors can all dump current into it and the currents simply add through Rf, with no interaction between sources — the summing amplifier, coming in Module 15, falls out for free. And a diagnostic gift: probe the − pin of any working inverting stage and you should read millivolts. If you read anything else, rule II has failed and the stage is broken — see below.

The non-inverting amplifier

Move the signal to the + input, and feed back a fraction of the output: Rf from output to the − input, Rg from the − input to ground — a plain Module 02 voltage divider hanging off the output. Rule I says the − input doesn't load the divider, so V₋ = Vout·Rg/(Rg+Rf). Rule II says the output makes V₋ = Vin. Set them equal and solve:

G = 1 + Rf / Rg the output is Vin "un-divided": whatever the divider keeps, the loop makes up — gain ≥ 1 always

With Rg = 10 k and Rf = 100 k the divider returns 1/11 of the output, so the output must be 11× the input: gain +11, no inversion. Notice the shape of the argument — the feedback divider reports a scaled-down copy of the output, and the op-amp raises the output until the report matches the input. Divide by eleven, multiply by eleven.

The win over the inverting topology: the signal now enters the op-amp's input pin directly, and by rule I that pin draws (essentially) nothing. Input impedance: gigohms — hundreds of megohms even for bipolar parts, teraohms for FET-input ones — instead of R₁'s few kilohms. When the source is feeble (a high-impedance sensor, a long probe, Module 02's divider that mustn't be loaded), non-inverting is the only respectful choice. The price: no virtual ground, no free summing node, gain can never dip below 1 — set Rf = 0 (or Rg = ∞) and you're back to the follower, which is exactly the limiting case G = 1.

When the rules break

Rule II begins "with negative feedback" and ends "whatever is necessary" — and each clause is a way to break it.

The rails. The output stage runs off the supplies; it cannot swing past them (most classic parts give up a volt or two short — our ±12 V amplifier saturates near ±11 V; "rail-to-rail" parts get within millivolts). Ask a gain-of-−10 stage for −15 V and the output travels to −11 V and stops. The moment it stops, the feedback current can no longer balance the input current, the virtual ground floats away from zero, the inputs are no longer equal — the amplifier is saturated, or "railed," and the transfer function goes flat. The signal comes out clipped: flat-topped where the demand exceeded the supply. Widget 2 below draws exactly this.

No feedback, or positive feedback. Remove Rf and the op-amp is open-loop: gain 10⁶ means the inputs' unavoidable microvolt-scale disagreement picks a rail, and the output sits there. Swap the inputs — feedback to the + terminal — and it's worse: now the output reinforces its own input error. Any wobble grows, the loop runs away, and the output latches hard at a rail (and if the input crosses far enough, snaps to the other one). This isn't a defective op-amp; it's a different circuit — the comparator, and done on purpose with a little positive feedback it becomes the Schmitt trigger, Module 16's opening act. The moral for now: an op-amp only obeys rule II if the net feedback is negative.

Current limits. "Whatever is necessary" also assumes the output can supply the current. A typical op-amp sources or sinks ~25 mA, politely limited to protect itself. Hang a 100 Ω load on an output trying to deliver 11 V (110 mA) and the output current-limits, the voltage sags, and — same story — the inputs unstick. Feedback makes the op-amp look ideal only within its muscle.

H&H habits
  • Golden rules first, algebra second. Before writing any equation, ask: where does the feedback hold the − input, and where must the input current go? Most op-amp circuits solve themselves in two sentences.
  • Inverting: G = −Rf/R₁ and Zin = R₁. Say both every time — the input impedance is the part beginners forget they chose.
  • Non-inverting: G = 1 + Rf/Rg, never less than 1; the follower is the Rf = 0 limit.
  • Keep feedback resistors roughly 1 k–1 MΩ. Much below 1 k and the feedback network itself becomes a heavy load on the output (and on the source, in the inverting case); much above 1 M and the "zero" input current stops being zero — tens of nanoamps of bias current across a megohm is tens of millivolts of error, and big resistors hiss (Johnson noise). Module 18 makes this quantitative.
  • The output is only as good as the feedback network. The op-amp contributes gain; the resistors contribute the accuracy. A gain-of-−10 built from 5% resistors is a 5%-ish amplifier no matter how heroic the op-amp. Spend your money on the ratio.
Thermostat analogy

A cruise control (or a thermostat) is a negative-feedback loop you already trust your life to. The engine can deliver a hundred kilowatts — enormous, imprecise muscle, the open-loop gain. The controller never meters that muscle directly; it just compares measured speed with requested speed and leans on the throttle in whatever direction shrinks the error. The system settles precisely where the error is zero — speedometer equals setpoint — and it holds there up hills and down, because any deviation instantly recruits the muscle against itself. That settling-to-zero-error is exactly rule II: the op-amp's inputs equalize because the output fights until they do.

And the feedback network? It's the gear ratio on the speedometer cable — it decides what "equal" means. Report the speed halved and the controller innocently drives at double the setpoint; report 1/11 of the output (our 10 k/100 k divider) and the output runs at 11× the input. The muscle sets what's possible; the reporting network sets what's true.

Where the analogy fails — instructively: real loops have finite speed and inertia. A cruise control that reacts too aggressively overshoots the setpoint and hunts around it; so does an op-amp, whose finite bandwidth and phase lag can turn negative feedback into positive at high frequency and make the "amplifier" oscillate. Compensation, bandwidth, and the price of the loop's dynamics are Module 18. And the engine analogy carries the rails with it: no controller can cruise at 200 km/h in a car whose engine tops out at 140.

Check yourself

Design bench

Golden rules, Ohm's law, and the 1 k–1 MΩ habit. Check the box when you've done it and verified against the solution.

Design an inverting amplifier with a gain of −5 whose input impedance is at least 20 kΩ. Choose R₁ and Rf, then explain what limits how high you'd push the values.

Show solution

The constraint does the choosing. For the inverting topology Zin = R₁, full stop — so the impedance spec pins R₁ = 20 kΩ (the minimum that satisfies it). Gain −5 then demands Rf = 5 × 20 k = 100 kΩ. Both are standard values and both sit comfortably in the 1 k–1 M window. Done in two lines — the golden rules turned a design problem into arithmetic.

Why not R₁ = 200 k, Rf = 1 M for a lordly 200 k input impedance? It "works," but you're buying Zin with error: bias current flowing in hundred-kilohm resistors leaves millivolt-scale offsets, and the Johnson noise of the resistors themselves grows with √R (Module 18). The inverting topology fundamentally trades input impedance against precision — need real input impedance? That's what Task 2's topology is for.

A sensor with a 100 kΩ source impedance delivers up to 0.9 V and needs a gain of 11. Pick the topology (and defend it against the alternative), then choose the resistor values.

Show solution

Non-inverting is mandatory. Module 02's rule: to leave a source undisturbed, look at it with an impedance much larger than its own. An inverting stage would present Zin = R₁ — and worse, the 100 kΩ source impedance sits in series with R₁ inside the gain equation. Try R₁ = 10 k, Rf = 110 k (nominal gain −11): actual gain = −110 k/(10 k + 100 k) = −1. Eleven times off, and it drifts with the sensor's impedance. The non-inverting input, by rule I, draws essentially nothing — the sensor doesn't even notice it's connected (Module 09's buffering job, done perfectly).

Values: G = 1 + Rf/Rg = 11 → Rf/Rg = 10: take Rg = 10 kΩ, Rf = 100 kΩ. Sanity-check the output: 0.9 V × 11 = 9.9 V — inside ±11 V rails with a little headroom to spare. (On a ±5 V supply this design would clip; always run the full-scale input against the rails before declaring victory.)

A lab partner breadboards the gain-of-−10 inverting amp, but the output sits pinned at +11 V no matter what the input does (tiny inputs, grounded input — nothing helps). Probing shows R₁ and Rf correct and the supplies fine, but Rf was accidentally returned to the + input, with the − input grounded. Explain the symptom, and state the general debugging rule this teaches.

Show solution

What happened: the feedback polarity is flipped, so the loop is positive. Suppose the output drifts a hair positive: the divider feeds a fraction of that to the + input, which drives the output more positive, which feeds back more still — the loop reinforces its own error at open-loop speed and slams into a rail, then holds itself there (the railed output keeps the + input biased on the same side). It latches: that's why the input hardly matters and why grounding it doesn't help. Rule II never applies — it is conditional on negative feedback, and this circuit is a comparator with hysteresis, not an amplifier (built on purpose, it's Module 16's Schmitt trigger).

The debugging rule: when an op-amp output is stuck at a rail, check the feedback polarity first — is the feedback network really returning to the − input? Then check that the demanded output is inside the rails, and that the inputs are inside their allowed range. Probing the two inputs tells you instantly: in a healthy feedback circuit they read equal (millivolts apart); a railed one shows them split apart — the golden rules used in reverse, as a diagnostic instrument.
