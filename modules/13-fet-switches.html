FET Switches & Analog Switching

The same transistor, two very different jobs: dumping amps into motors and LEDs without breaking a sweat, and passing fragile analog signals without leaving a fingerprint on them. AoE 3rd ed. §3.4–3.5.

Why this matters

Module 12 introduced the FET as a voltage-controlled device with an insulated gate. This module — the last of Part II — puts it to work at its two signature jobs. The first is switching power: a logic pin that weighs nothing commanding an amp or ten into a motor, a relay coil, a strip of LEDs. BJTs can do this too, but the FET does it with no base current to supply and milliohms of on-resistance, which is why essentially every modern power switch is a MOSFET. The second job is stranger and more exclusive: switching signals — routing a delicate analog voltage from here to there, connecting and disconnecting it on command, without touching it. No offset added, no current stolen, no distortion smeared on. The FET is the only device that does this second job well, and it's worth understanding exactly why — because the reasons tell you both when to reach for an analog switch and when it will quietly betray you.

Physics background — why a FET makes a good analog switch

Recall from Module 12 that with plenty of gate drive and only a small drain–source voltage, the FET sits in the triode (linear) region: the channel is just a slab of conductive silicon — a resistor, RDS(on). And a genuinely remarkable resistor, for switching purposes: it is bidirectional (current flows either way — there is no junction in the path insisting on a direction), and it has no offset voltage. At zero current the drop is exactly zero. Compare the competition: a saturated BJT never gets below VCE(sat) ≈ 0.1–0.2 V — a built-in error stamped onto every signal it passes — and a diode demands its 0.6 V toll before conducting at all. The FET channel asks for nothing; a small signal passes through as if through a plain 50 Ω resistor, because it is a plain 50 Ω resistor.

Better still, the control terminal is insulated: the gate injects no current into the signal path. Almost. The gate is a capacitor plate facing the channel, and when the gate voltage slews, a squirt of charge couples capacitively into the signal — the charge injection we'll meet in the gotchas section. Small, but not zero, and it will matter exactly when you can least afford it.

Now the catch. Ron depends on the gate overdrive, VGS − Vth — and for an analog switch, the source terminal is the signal. Gate held at VDD, signal wandering up and down: VGS = VDD − Vsig, so Ron varies with the very signal it is passing. That's the Ron flatness problem — a signal-dependent series resistance, which is a polite name for distortion. The classic fix is the complementary transmission gate: an N-FET and a P-FET in parallel, gates driven oppositely, so whichever device is starving for overdrive, the other is feasting. We'll build it below.

Switching power — the low-side cell

Here is the standard cell from Module 12, worth engraving: logic pin → small gate resistor → N-channel MOSFET, source grounded, load from drain up to the positive supply. Logic HIGH puts the full logic swing across gate–source (source is at ground, so VGS = Vlogic exactly — the great convenience of low-side switching), the channel drops to milliohms, and the load sees essentially the whole supply. Logic LOW, VGS = 0, and the FET is an open circuit but for nanoamps of leakage. The gate resistor (47–330 Ω, uncritical) isn't there to set a current — the gate draws none at DC — it damps the ringing between the driver and the gate capacitance and blunts the charging-current spike drawn from the logic pin at each edge.

If the load is inductive — motor, relay coil, solenoid — the Module 04 rule applies with full force: the coil's current cannot stop instantly, and at switch-off it will generate whatever voltage it takes to keep flowing. Give it somewhere to go: a flyback diode across the load, cathode to the supply, sits idle while the switch is on and catches the coil's current at turn-off, clamping the drain to one diode drop above the rail instead of letting it spike to FET-destroying heights.

And one paragraph on PWM, because this cell is how power is controlled, not just switched: to run that motor at 40% speed, don't drop the extra voltage across a pass element — switch fully on and fully off, fast, with a 40% duty cycle, and let the motor's inertia (or an LC filter) average it. The Module 01 callback makes the case: P = V·I in the switch. Fully on, V across the FET is millivolts — P tiny. Fully off, I is nanoamps — P tiny. The switch only visits the deadly high-V·I middle ground for the few nanoseconds of each transition. A linear controller dropping 7 V at 1 A dissipates 7 W and needs a heatsink; the same FET switched hard dissipates I²R = (1 A)²·50 mΩ = 50 mW and doesn't get warm. That hundredfold difference is the entire premise of switching regulators, motor drives, and Class-D amplifiers.

High-side switching — when the load demands a real ground

Low-side switching is the default because it's easy. But notice what it does to the load: when the switch is off, the load's bottom end floats — the load is still tied to the positive rail, and its "ground" terminal dangles. For a dumb LED strip, who cares. For anything with a second connection to the outside world — a sensor sharing an ADC, a module with a communication line, anything with a chassis connection — a floating or bouncing ground reference is poison: signals leak through unintended paths, and even when "off" the load sits at supply potential, ready to find sneak return routes through those other connections. When the load needs a solid, permanent ground, you must switch the high side.

The recipe is the P-channel mirror image of the standard cell: P-FET with source to the positive rail, load from drain to ground. Gate pulled up to the rail = off (VGS = 0); gate pulled down = on (VGS goes several volts negative). The mental speed bump is that P-FET logic is inverted — gate LOW turns it ON — and there's a practical snag: a 3.3 V logic pin cannot work the gate directly. "Off" means gate at +12 V, and your microcontroller pin must never be dragged to +12. So we level-shift with the smallest possible circuit: an NPN with its emitter grounded, base driven from logic through a resistor, collector to the P-FET gate, and a pull-up resistor from gate to the +12 V rail. Logic HIGH → NPN saturates → gate yanked to ~0.2 V → VGS ≈ −11.8 V → P-FET hard on. Logic LOW → NPN off → pull-up parks the gate at +12 → VGS = 0 → off. Two inversions in a row (NPN inverts, P-FET's gate sense inverts) cancel out, and logic HIGH means load ON, as nature intended. The widget below draws it live.

Can an N-FET switch the high side? Only if you can hold its gate above the positive rail by a full VGS — which is exactly what charge-pump "high-side driver" ICs and bootstrap circuits exist to do, and all we'll say about them here.

Analog switches — the transmission gate

Now the second job. Take a small N-FET, put the signal on one channel terminal and the load on the other, drive the gate to VDD to close the switch, to 0 (or below) to open it. For signals near ground this works beautifully: overdrive is huge, Ron is low and steady. But watch what happens as the signal climbs. The FET's effective source is the channel terminal, riding on the signal, so the overdrive is VDD − Vsig − Vth — shrinking as the signal rises. Ron climbs, gently at first, then steeply, and as Vsig approaches VDD − Vth the switch simply stops conducting. A single N-FET switch passes low voltages well and dies near the top of the range. A single P-FET (gate driven to 0 to turn on) is the exact mirror image: happy at the top, dead near ground.

Ron ≈ 1 / [k·(VGS − Vth)] triode-region channel resistance — inversely proportional to gate overdrive, and for an analog switch the overdrive depends on the signal

You can hear the fix coming: put both in parallel, gates driven to opposite rails, and you have the complementary transmission gate. Where the N-FET is starving, the P-FET is fully enhanced, and vice versa; the parallel combination (Module 01!) is dominated by whichever is lower, and the result is a flattish "bathtub" of Ron across the whole supply range. This is the circuit inside the classic 4066 quad bilateral switch — four transmission gates with their opposite-phase gate drivers in one package — and inside the 4051 family of analog multiplexers, which are just trees of transmission gates with an address decoder: three digital pins select which of eight analog paths is connected. Route audio, chop signals, share one ADC among eight sensors — all the same trick.

Analog-switch gotchas

Analog switches are honest parts, but their datasheets confess four sins you must design around:

1. Ron is not zero. Tens to hundreds of ohms, and (as the widget shows) not constant. That resistance forms a voltage divider (Module 02) with whatever load the switch drives: 100 Ω of switch into a 10 kΩ load is a 1% error — and a signal-dependent 1%, which is distortion, not just gain error. The cure is the Thévenin habit: drive high-impedance loads. Into 100 kΩ the same switch contributes 0.1%; into an op-amp follower's near-infinite input, essentially nothing.

2. Charge injection. The gate must slew a full rail-to-rail swing to operate the switch, and the gate–channel capacitance couples a small packet of charge — a picocoulomb or so — straight into the signal path at every transition. Into a stiff low-impedance source it vanishes; into a small hold capacitor it becomes a voltage step, ΔV = Q/C. This is the classic plague of the sample-and-hold (a preview of Part III's data-conversion circuits: switch closes, cap tracks the signal; switch opens, cap holds it for the ADC). A 1 pC injection into 200 pF is a 5 mV glitch — a full LSB at 10 bits. Bigger caps glitch less (ΔV ∝ 1/Chold) but charge slower; complementary switches help too, since the N and P gates slew in opposite directions and their injections partially cancel.

3. Off-state leakage. An open switch isn't open — nanoamps trickle through. Irrelevant into a 10 kΩ load; deadly into that same hold capacitor, where 1 nA drains 1 mV from 1 nF every millisecond (Module 03's I = C·dV/dt, running in reverse on you).

4. The signal must stay within the switch's supply rails. The channel sits in a substrate tied to the supplies; drag the signal beyond a rail and substrate junctions forward-bias — at best the switch conducts when it shouldn't, at worst the chip latches up. A ±5 V audio signal through a 0–5 V-supplied 4066 is a bug you only ship once.

Plumbing analogy — three kinds of valve

A power switch is the main water valve: its job is fully open or fully shut, and both states are cheap to hold — open, the water barely notices it; shut, nothing flows, nothing is lost. What's expensive is holding it half-open: the valve itself eats the pressure drop, roaring and heating. That half-open state is linear regulation, and PWM is the trick of never lingering there — snap between the two cheap states and let the average do the regulating.

An analog switch is a clean, food-grade valve in a sampling line at a bottling plant. Its duty isn't moving volume — it's passing a small taste of the product unchanged. It must not flavor what passes (offset voltage), must not restrict it (Ron), and must not contaminate it when it operates (charge injection — the puff of air the valve stem pushes into the line every time it moves).

A transmission gate is two such valves in parallel, angled opposite ways, so that whatever the line pressure, at least one of them is always fully open — the flow always has one easy path.

Where the analogy fails: water valves don't leak backward through their stems the way an off FET leaks nanoamps; the "puff" of charge injection scales with the speed of gate slew and the smallness of what's downstream, which has no clean plumbing counterpart; and no water valve stops working just because the line pressure got within Vth of the supply.

H&H rules of thumb
  • Low-side switch by default — it's the simple, full-gate-drive cell. Go high-side when the load demands a solid ground reference (shared signals, sensors, anything with a second path to the world).
  • P-FET logic is inverted: gate LOW = on. With an NPN level shifter in front, the two inversions cancel and logic HIGH = load ON. Say the chain out loud until it stops surprising you.
  • Analog switch Ron should feed a load ≥ 100× its value — 100 Ω of switch wants ≥ 10 kΩ, comfortably 100 kΩ or an op-amp input.
  • Watch the signal range against the switch's supply — the switch only works (and only survives) between its own rails, and single-FET switches die a Vth short of the rail.
  • Charge injection ∝ 1/Chold: bigger hold caps glitch less but settle slower (τ = RonC). Every sample-and-hold is a negotiation between those two.

Check yourself

Design bench

Real tasks, pencil-and-paper (or head) only. Check the box when you've done it and verified against the solution.

A 12 V, 2 A LED strip must be switched from a 3.3 V logic pin, and the strip's ground must stay solidly grounded (it shares a metal channel with other electronics). Design the complete high-side switch: choose the topology, all resistor values, and verify both states — including what holds the switch off when the logic pin is low, and the power dissipated in the FET when on (assume RDS(on) = 50 mΩ).

Show solution

Topology: P-FET, source to +12 V, drain to the strip, strip to ground. Gate: 10 kΩ pull-up to +12 V, and down to the collector of a small NPN (2N3904 class), emitter grounded, base fed from the logic pin through 4.7 kΩ. Pick a P-FET with VGS rating ≥ ±20 V and RDS(on) specified at VGS = −10 V or less (we'll give it −11.8).

ON state (logic HIGH): base current ≈ (3.3 − 0.7)/4.7k = 0.55 mA. Collector current is only the pull-up's 12/10k = 1.2 mA, so even β = 10 saturates it hopelessly hard — the NPN drags the gate to ≈ 0.2 V, giving VGS = 0.2 − 12 = −11.8 V: fully enhanced, safely inside ±20 V. Housekeeping costs: ~14 mW in the pull-up (11.8²/10k) and ~1.4 mW in the base resistor — negligible. FET dissipation: P = I²R = 2² × 0.05 = 0.2 W — warm but fine for a SOT-23 power FET with a bit of copper pour; no heatsink.

OFF state (logic LOW): NPN off; its collector leakage (nA) through 10 kΩ drops microvolts, so the gate sits at 12.000 V and VGS = 0 — the pull-up guarantees off, even with the micro unpowered or the pin floating at reset. That guarantee is the pull-up's real job; never leave a power FET's gate to float.

A battery-powered board switches a sensor's power with a low-side N-FET (to save sleep current). The sensor's analog output goes to an ADC that other circuits share. Symptom: the sensor's readings are offset high by a few millivolts, and the offset changes when the sensor's own supply current changes. The sensor draws 20 mA; the small switching FET has RDS(on) = 0.5 Ω. Diagnose the offset, put a number on it (the ADC is 10-bit, 5 V full scale), and propose the fix.

Show solution

Diagnosis: the low-side FET sits between the sensor's ground pin and the board's real ground, so the sensor's entire supply current flows through RDS(on). The sensor's "ground" is bounced up by I·R = 20 mA × 0.5 Ω = 10 mV — and the sensor's output, referenced to its own lifted ground, arrives at the ADC (referenced to real ground) offset by that amount. At 5/1024 = 4.88 mV per LSB, that's about 2 LSB — and it wanders with supply current, so you can't even calibrate it out. Exactly the "load loses its ground reference" failure the high-side section warned about.

Fix: switch the sensor's high side (P-FET + level shifter from Task 1, scaled down) so its ground pin ties directly and permanently to board ground. Where high-side isn't available, the damage can be reduced by careful grounding — sense the sensor's output differentially, or route a dedicated sense/ground trace to the sensor's ground pin so no load current flows in the measurement path. That's Kelvin sensing and star grounding — Module 30's whole subject, previewed here by a 0.5 Ω villain.

A sample-and-hold uses an analog switch (Ron = 100 Ω) to charge a hold capacitor, which must then hold its voltage for 1 ms while a 10-bit, 5 V ADC does other work. Total leakage off the cap (switch + ADC input) is 1 nA. Choose C for less than 1 LSB of droop, then compute the acquisition time (take 5τ) — and describe what you're trading away as C grows.

Show solution

Droop: 1 LSB = 5 V/1024 = 4.88 mV. The cap loses charge at I = 1 nA for 1 ms, so ΔV = I·t/C, and C ≥ I·t/ΔV = (1 nA × 1 ms)/4.88 mV ≈ 0.2 nF (0.205 nF exactly — use 220 pF or larger).

Acquisition: τ = RonC = 100 Ω × 0.205 nF = 20.5 ns; 5τ ≈ 100 ns. (Honesty note: 5τ settles to 0.7% of the step, ≈ 7 LSB at 10 bits — to settle within ½ LSB you actually need ln(2048) ≈ 7.6τ, about 156 ns. Cheap here, but the habit of matching settling depth to converter resolution will save you in Part III.)

The trade: minimum-C isn't optimum-C. That 0.2 nF cap also receives the switch's charge injection: 1 pC into 0.205 nF is a 4.9 mV glitch — a full LSB, right at your error budget. Grow C to 10 nF and both droop (0.1 mV) and injection glitch (0.1 mV) become negligible… but acquisition stretches to 5τ = 5 µs, capping your sample rate. Every sample-and-hold lives on this seesaw: bigger C holds cleaner, smaller C acquires faster, and the design point is wherever your droop, glitch, and speed budgets balance.
