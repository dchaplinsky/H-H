Comparators & Schmitt Triggers

The circuit that turns an analog voltage into a yes-or-no answer — and the positive-feedback trick that keeps the answer from stuttering. AoE 3rd ed. §4.3.2 & §12.3.

Why this matters

The world is analog; decisions are digital. Somewhere in almost every instrument a smoothly varying voltage has to become a clean yes or no: is the battery low? is the heatsink too hot? has the sine wave crossed zero yet? is this logic input a 1 or a 0? Every one of those questions is answered by a comparator — a circuit whose entire job is to report which of its two inputs is higher. It is the border crossing between Part III's analog world and the digital circuits ahead, and it appears inside ADCs, timers, reset supervisors, switching regulators, and every logic gate's input stage.

And here is the part that separates working designs from bench curiosities: every real comparator needs hysteresis. A single threshold plus real-world noise equals an output that buzzes like an angry wasp exactly when the decision matters most — slowly crossing the line. The fix, the Schmitt trigger, is our first deliberate use of positive feedback, after two modules of carefully cultivated negative feedback. Same op-amp topology, opposite sign, completely different personality: instead of a smooth linear servo you get a snap-action switch with memory.

Physics background — an op-amp with the feedback removed

A comparator is conceptually just an op-amp running open-loop. Recall the numbers from Module 14: open-loop gain of 10⁵–10⁶. With no feedback network to tame it, a difference of a few microvolts between V₊ and V₋ is amplified into volts — the output slams into whichever supply rail the sign of the difference selects and stays there. The "linear region" between the rails is only tens of microvolts wide: for any practical input, the output is a two-state device. V₊ > V₋ → output high; V₊ < V₋ → output low. That's the entire truth table.

So why do dedicated comparator ICs exist at all? Because an op-amp is built for negative feedback and pays for it when you take the feedback away. (1) Speed: an op-amp's internal phase-compensation capacitor — the thing that keeps it stable in a feedback loop — deliberately slows it down, and its saturated output stage takes microseconds to crawl back off a rail. A comparator has no compensation capacitor and is designed to bang between states in nanoseconds. (2) Logic-friendly outputs: a comparator's output stage swings between ground and a logic level, not to within a volt or two of split supplies. (3) Many comparators offer an open-collector output — the output "transistor" is Module 08's saturated BJT switch with the collector left floating for you: it can pull the output down hard, but going up is your job, via an external pull-up resistor to whatever logic voltage you like. More on why that's a feature, not a cost-cut, below.

The naked comparator

The minimal decision circuit: put a reference voltage on one input and the signal on the other. The reference almost always comes from Module 02's favorite subcircuit — a voltage divider. A 10 kΩ/10 kΩ divider from a 5 V rail parks the reference at 2.5 V; the comparator then reports "above" or "below" with a rail-to-rail output:

V₊ > V₋  →  Vout = high rail      V₊ < V₋  →  Vout = low rail with the signal on V₋ and the reference on V₊, the output is high while the signal is below the reference — the inverting arrangement we'll standardize on

On paper this works perfectly. On the bench it fails in a specific, maddening way.

The chatter problem

Real signals carry noise — thermal hiss, mains pickup, ripple from the switching supply two boards over. Millivolts, usually; harmless, usually. But a comparator has microvolt sensitivity and no short-term memory. As a slowly rising signal approaches the threshold, the noise riding on it crosses the line dozens of times before the underlying trend finally carries it clear. Each tiny crossing is a full-scale output transition: instead of one clean edge, the output emits a burst of hash. The slower the signal, the longer it loiters inside the noise band around the threshold, and the worse the buzz. If that output is clocking a counter, you count one sunrise as fifty. If it's driving a relay, the contacts machine-gun themselves to death.

Positive feedback on purpose

The cure for chatter is to give the comparator a memory: after it decides, move the threshold away from the signal. Once the output snaps high, raise the bar; once it snaps low, lower it. Now a noise wiggle that re-crosses the old threshold finds the goalposts gone — to undo the decision, the signal must traverse the whole gap between the two thresholds. That gap is the hysteresis, and the circuit is the Schmitt trigger.

The implementation costs one resistor. Take the inverting comparator — signal into V₋, divider reference into V₊ — and add a feedback resistor Rh from the output back to V₊. Because the feedback returns to the non-inverting input, it is positive: the output state itself tugs the reference in the direction that entrenches that state.

Here is our canonical circuit for the whole module. Comparator powered from 5 V with a push-pull output swinging essentially 0 V / 5 V; reference set by R1 (from +5 V) and R2 (to ground); Rh from output to the V₊ node. Three resistors meet at V₊, so the node voltage is a three-way weighted average — and superposition (Module 02, again) hands us each threshold in one line. Looking into the node, each source drives it through its own resistor with the other two resistors going to AC-ground:

VT = 5 V · (R₂∥Rh)/(R₁ + R₂∥Rh)  +  Vout · (R₁∥R₂)/(Rh + R₁∥R₂) superposition at the V₊ node: the rail's share plus the output's share

With the output low (Vout = 0) the second term vanishes and the node sits at the lower threshold; with the output high (Vout = 5 V) the second term adds on top and the node sits at the upper threshold:

VT− = 5 · (R₂∥Rh)/(R₁ + R₂∥Rh)      VT+ = VT− + 5 · (R₁∥R₂)/(Rh + R₁∥R₂) two thresholds from one node; hysteresis ΔV = VT+ − VT− = Vswing · (R₁∥R₂)/(Rh + R₁∥R₂)

Numbers, and a cross-check. Take R₁ = R₂ = 10 kΩ, Rh = 100 kΩ. Then R₂∥Rh = 9.09 kΩ, so VT− = 5 × 9.09/19.09 = 2.381 V; and R₁∥R₂ = 5 kΩ, so the output's contribution is 5 × 5/105 = 0.238 V, giving VT+ = 2.619 V and ΔV = 238 mV. Never trust one method: write KCL at the node directly. Currents in = currents out: (5 − V)/R₁ + (Vout − V)/Rh = V/R₂, so V = (5/R₁ + Vout/Rh) / (1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/Rh). Plug in Vout = 0: V = (5×10⁻⁴)/(2.1×10⁻⁴) = 2.381 V. Plug in Vout = 5: V = (5.5×10⁻⁴)/(2.1×10⁻⁴) = 2.619 V. Same answers to the last digit — superposition and the node equation are the same physics wearing different notation.

Why does the transition snap? Regeneration. The instant the signal edges past the threshold, the output starts to move; the moving output drags V₊ away from the signal through Rh, which increases the input difference, which drives the output harder — a self-reinforcing avalanche limited only by the comparator's own speed. Negative feedback fights change; positive feedback finishes it. The output spends essentially zero time in between states, no matter how lazily the input crawls.

The non-inverting flavor, for completeness: put the signal into V₊ through a series resistor Rin, a fixed reference on V₋, and Rh from output to V₊ as before. Now the signal and the output share the V₊ node in an Rin/Rh tug-of-war, and the signal must overpower the output's vote to trip: with the reference at mid-rail the thresholds land symmetrically at Vref ± Vref·Rin/Rh, for a hysteresis of ΔV = Vswing·Rin/Rh (check: Rin = 10 k, Rh = 100 k, 2.5 V reference → trips at 2.25 and 2.75 V; the node equation confirms V₊ hits exactly 2.5 V at both). It's handy when the signal's own source impedance can serve as Rin (Design bench Task 1 flirts with this), but we'll keep the inverting version as the module's canonical circuit and nail its math cold rather than juggling both.

Household analogy — the thermostat's dead band

Your home thermostat is a Schmitt trigger with a furnace for an output stage. It does not hold the house at exactly 20 °C — it kicks the furnace on when the room drifts down to 19 °C and off when it climbs to 21 °C. That 2-degree dead band is hysteresis, and it exists for exactly our reason: without it, the thermostat would sit at the threshold and cycle the furnace every few seconds as tiny drafts — thermal noise, literally — wobbled the sensor across the line. Chatter, with a gas bill.

The toggle light switch on your wall makes the other half of the argument: its over-center spring is mechanical positive feedback. Push it past the balance point and the spring itself finishes the throw — snap. It cannot hover between states, which is precisely what you want from anything driving a set of contacts (or a logic input).

Where the analogy fails, as they all do: the dead band isn't free — the room genuinely wanders two degrees, and hysteresis likewise costs you threshold precision: you no longer know "the" switching point, only a band. That's an engineering trade, not a flaw. And real comparators have offset voltages and response-time limits that the spring picture hides; the snap is fast, not instantaneous.

Sizing the hysteresis

How wide should the band be? Squeeze it between two constraints:

  • Wider than the noise. If peak noise is ±n, the band must exceed 2n or noise alone can carry the signal across both thresholds — that's the ≥ 2× peak noise rule. And since bad days are noisier than average days, margin beyond the minimum is cheap insurance.
  • Narrower than the signal. The signal must actually reach both thresholds or the output never switches at all. A sensor that swings 300 mV can't drive a 400 mV band — the comparator would make one decision at power-up and retire.

Worked numbers on the canonical circuit: suppose the signal arrives with ±50 mV of noise. The rule's minimum is 2 × 50 = 100 mV; take a comfortable 2× margin on top and aim for ΔV ≈ 200 mV. With R₁ = R₂ = 10 kΩ, R₁∥R₂ = 5 kΩ and ΔV = 5 V · 5k/(Rh + 5k). Solving for 200 mV gives Rh = 120 kΩ — an E24 value, no less — and the thresholds land at a satisfying 2.40 V and 2.60 V exactly (check: 1/R₁+1/R₂+1/Rh = 2.083×10⁻⁴ S; 5×10⁻⁴/2.083×10⁻⁴ = 2.400; add 5/120k ÷ 2.083×10⁻⁴ = 0.200). Noise of ±50 mV now has to fake a 200 mV excursion to cause a false switch — it can't.

Open-collector outputs — bring your own pull-up

Classic comparators (the LM311, the ubiquitous LM393/LM339 family) don't have a push-pull output at all. The output pin is the bare collector of an internal NPN — Module 08's saturated switch with the collector resistor omitted on purpose. The transistor can sink current and pull the pin low, hard; when it turns off, the pin doesn't go high — it goes nowhere. It simply floats until you connect a pull-up resistor to the logic rail of your choice. No pull-up, no output — the number-one LM393 support question of all time is a perfectly fine circuit missing one 10 kΩ resistor.

Why build it this way? Two genuinely good reasons:

  • Level shifting for free. The comparator can run from ±15 V analog supplies while its pull-up goes to 3.3 V — the output swings 0–3.3 V, perfectly matched to the microcontroller reading it. The output logic level is set by the pull-up's rail, not the comparator's supply.
  • Wired-OR. Tie several open-collector outputs to one pull-up and the shared line goes low if any comparator pulls it down — a free OR (of active-low flags) with no gate. That's how a window comparator (too-high OR too-low → alarm) is built from two LM393 halves and three resistors.

One honest footnote for Schmitt duty: with an open-collector part, the "high" state reaches V₊ through the pull-up, so Rp is in series with Rh during half the cycle. Keep Rp ≪ Rh (1 kΩ against our 100 kΩ shifts VT+ by about a millivolt) or fold it into the arithmetic. Rules of thumb are for starting, not for shipping.

The 555, previewed in one paragraph

Open the hood of the immortal 555 timer and you find this module twice over: a three-resistor divider (three equal 5 kΩ resistors — hence, by legend, the name) pins two references at ⅓ VCC and ⅔ VCC; two comparators watch the trigger and threshold pins against them; and their outputs set and reset a flip-flop that drives the output stage and a discharge transistor. The flip-flop is the hysteresis, made explicit as a memory element: the 555 is a Schmitt trigger with its two thresholds pulled a full third of the supply apart. Charge a capacitor between those thresholds and you have an oscillator — which is exactly where Module 19 picks up the story.

H&H rules of thumb
  • No comparator without hysteresis — unless the signal is genuinely fast and clean (a logic-level edge, say). Slow or noisy signals through a naked comparator chatter. Always.
  • Size the band generously: hysteresis ≥ 2× the peak noise you expect, while staying comfortably inside the signal's swing.
  • Open-collector needs a pull-up — no pull-up, no output. Check this first when a comparator "doesn't work". And keep Rp small next to Rh.
  • Don't use op-amps as comparators: the compensation capacitor that makes them docile in feedback makes them slow open-loop, and recovery from output saturation adds microseconds of sluggish limbo per edge.
  • Don't use comparators as op-amps: no internal compensation means a comparator inside a negative-feedback loop oscillates merrily. Each part does one job.

Check yourself

Design bench

Pencil, paper, and the two threshold formulas. Check the box when you've worked the task and compared against the solution.

A 12 V lead-acid battery powers a small instrument that also has a clean 5 V logic rail and a comparator with a 2.5 V-ish reference network. Design a monitor: an LED turns on when the battery sags below 11.0 V and doesn't turn off again until it recovers above 11.5 V (so it can't flicker as the load pulses). Scale the battery down with a divider, build the thresholds with our canonical Schmitt, and verify the numbers honestly.

Show solution

Two sub-designs: a divider to bring 11–11.5 V into comparator territory, and a Schmitt whose two thresholds land on the divided values. Work backwards from the ratio the battery spec demands: VT+/VT− must equal 11.5/11 = 23/22. For our Schmitt that ratio is (from the node equation) VT+/VT− = 1 + R₁/Rh — so we need Rh = 22·R₁. Take R₁ = R₂ = 10 kΩ, Rh = 220 kΩ (all E24). Node math: ΣG = 10⁻⁴+10⁻⁴+1/220k = 2.045×10⁻⁴ S, so VT− = 5×10⁻⁴/2.045×10⁻⁴ = 2.444 V and VT+ = 5.227×10⁻⁴/2.045×10⁻⁴ = 2.556 V (ΔV = 111 mV; ratio 2.556/2.444 = 23/22 exactly, as designed).

Now the divider ratio: k = 2.444/11.0 = 2/9 exactly. With the lower resistor 10 kΩ, the upper must be 35 kΩ — build it as 33 kΩ + 2 kΩ in series (both E24) over 10 kΩ. Check both ends: 11.0 × 10/45 = 2.444 V = VT− ✓ (LED on), and 11.5 × 10/45 = 2.556 V = VT+ ✓ (LED off). The spec is hit exactly — before 1% resistor tolerance, which moves each point by a couple of percent; the hysteresis ordering, though, is guaranteed by topology.

Loose ends an honest designer ties off: the divider drains 12/45k ≈ 267 µA forever (3 mW — negligible on a lead-acid, worth rethinking on a coin cell); the tap's Thévenin impedance is 35k∥10k ≈ 7.8 kΩ, fine against a comparator's nanoamp inputs (Module 02's 10× rule, satisfied by four orders of magnitude). LED drive: the tap goes to V₋, so the output sits high when the battery is low — with a push-pull comparator, LED + 470 Ω from output to ground gives (5−2)/470 ≈ 6 mA. With an LM393, remember the output only pulls low: either re-plumb so "battery low" is the low state, or add Module 08's NPN buffer — and note that whatever hangs on the output node changes the high level that Rh feeds back, so re-check the thresholds with the real VOH.

A photoresistor (LDR) reads roughly 5 kΩ in daylight and hundreds of kΩ in darkness, passing through ~10 kΩ around dusk. Wire it as the top half of a divider (Module 02) with 10 kΩ below, feed the tap to our canonical Schmitt (10k/10k reference, 100 kΩ feedback, thresholds 2.381/2.619 V), and drive an LED. At what LDR resistances does the light switch on and off? Why is hysteresis essential here — and what does too much of it cost?

Show solution

The tap voltage is 5 · 10k/(RLDR + 10k): high in daylight (LDR small), falling as night comes. Tap to V₋ means the output goes high — LED on — when the tap drops below a threshold. Falling tap trips at VT− = 2.381 V: solve 5·10k/(R+10k) = 2.381 → R = 21.0k − 10k = 11.0 kΩ. Light turns off next morning when the rising tap crosses VT+ = 2.619 V → R = 19.09k − 10k = 9.09 kΩ. So: on when the LDR darkens past 11 kΩ, off only after it brightens past 9.1 kΩ.

Why it matters: dusk is the slowest signal in this module — the tap crawls through the threshold over many minutes while clouds, headlights and the LED's own glow (optical feedback! mount it away from the sensor) add noise. A naked comparator would flicker for half an hour. The ~240 mV band means the ambient light must change by a solid, deliberate amount to reverse the decision.

The trade: hysteresis here is annoyance, in lux. Too wide a band and the lamp ignores real twilight — on absurdly late, off absurdly late — exactly the thermostat's comfort-versus-cycling bargain. Size it to straddle the flicker sources (minutes of cloud noise), not the phenomenon (the day/night swing). If flicker persists, an RC low-pass on the tap (Module 03) slows the noise before the decision — filtering and hysteresis stack politely.

A function generator's 1 kHz, 5 Vpp sine must become a square wave marking each zero crossing. Why should the comparator get minimal — but not zero — hysteresis? Estimate the timing error a 50 mV hysteresis band introduces at the crossings, and express it as a fraction of the period.

Show solution

Zero, because timing accuracy is the whole product: every millivolt of threshold offset shifts the reported crossing. Not zero, because a generator output still carries a few millivolts of noise and the sine spends its steepest-but-still-finite time near zero — a naked comparator would double-trigger. So: the smallest band that clears the noise, per the 2× rule. (This is the one case the rule panel concedes: a truly fast, clean signal can go without.)

Timing error. The sine is v(t) = 2.5 · sin(2πft) volts (5 Vpp → 2.5 V amplitude), and its slope at a zero crossing is the steepest it ever gets: dv/dt = 2π·f·A = 2π × 1000 × 2.5 ≈ 15,700 V/s. The two thresholds sit 50 mV apart, so the rising and falling switch points are separated in voltage by ΔV and in time by

dt = ΔV / (dv/dt) = 0.05 / 15,708 ≈ 3.2 µs

— or ±1.6 µs per edge if the band is centered on zero. Against the 1 ms period that's 0.32% of a cycle, about 1.1° of phase (0.57° per edge, centered). Whether that's negligible or fatal depends entirely on the job — timestamping mains cycles, fine; phase-measuring instrument, budget for it or shrink ΔV toward the noise floor. Note the scaling: dt ∝ ΔV/(f·A), so slower or smaller signals hurt more — which is exactly why the chatter lab's lazy 1 Hz sine was such easy prey.
