Capacitors & RC Circuits

The first component with a memory: a part that cares not about voltage but about how fast voltage changes — and the exponential curve that governs every charge and discharge. AoE 3rd ed. §1.4.

Why this matters

Pick up any circuit board and count the parts: after resistors, capacitors are the most common thing on it — often by a wide margin. They set timing (how long before the reset releases), do filtering (smoothing a rectified supply, killing noise), handle coupling (passing the signal, blocking the DC it rides on) and decoupling (that forest of little 100 nF parts next to every chip). One number, the time constant τ = RC, runs everything from a camera flash charging up to the debounce delay on the keyboard you're typing on. Master τ and you can read half the passive circuitry ever drawn.

Physics background

A capacitor is embarrassingly simple: two conducting plates that don't touch, separated by an insulator (the dielectric). Push charge onto one plate and it repels an equal charge off the other; you end up with +Q on one plate, −Q on the other, and an electric field stretched across the gap. That field is where the energy lives — the capacitor is a little spring wound out of electrostatics.

The charge stored is proportional to the voltage across the plates. The constant of proportionality is the capacitance:

C = Q / V farads = coulombs per volt; 1 F is enormous — real parts run pF to mF

Geometry sets C: for parallel plates, C = εA/d — bigger plate area A, or a smaller gap d, means more charge stored per volt. The dielectric's permittivity ε multiplies the result: its molecules polarize in the field, partially cancelling it, so you can cram more charge on at the same voltage. Good dielectrics buy you factors of 4–10,000× over vacuum, which is why real capacitors are thin films and ceramics rolled or stacked into tiny packages.

Charging the spring stores energy:

E = ½ C V² joules; each extra coulomb is pushed on against a higher voltage, hence the ½·(...)² shape

Now the law that gives the capacitor its personality. Differentiate Q = CV with respect to time, and remember current is dQ/dt:

I = C · dV/dt current flows only while the voltage is changing

One subtlety worth internalizing: no electron ever crosses the gap. The "current through" a capacitor is really charge piling onto one plate while equal charge drains off the other — from outside the two terminals it is indistinguishable from a through-current (Maxwell called the field's share of it displacement current), but inside, the dielectric stays an insulator. That's why a capacitor can pass a signal while blocking DC completely.

I = C·dV/dt, unpacked

Read the law slowly — it is the whole module in one line:

  • Steady voltage → zero current. If dV/dt = 0, then I = 0. At DC, once everything settles, a capacitor is an open circuit. It simply isn't there.
  • Fast change → big current. Try to yank the voltage across a capacitor quickly and it demands a proportionally huge current. Try to change it instantly and it demands infinite current — which no real circuit supplies. Conclusion: the voltage across a capacitor cannot jump.
  • So, instantaneously, a capacitor behaves like a voltage source holding whatever voltage it currently has. In the first moment after you throw a switch, replace every capacitor in your head with a battery at its present voltage (an uncharged one is a 0 V source — i.e. a short circuit). Long after the switch, replace it with an open circuit. Those two limiting pictures solve most switch-flipping problems before you write any equations.

In short: a capacitor fights voltage change, and the fight is what makes it useful.

RC charging — the exponential everyone must own

Connect a battery V through a resistor R to a capacitor C. What does the capacitor voltage VC do? At the first instant VC = 0, so the full battery voltage sits across R and the current is V/R — the maximum it will ever be. That current charges C, so VC rises… which leaves less voltage across R, so the current shrinks, so VC rises more slowly, which shrinks the current further… The chase never quite ends: the charging current is proportional to the remaining distance to the target. A quantity whose rate of change is proportional to how far it still has to go is the definition of an exponential approach:

VC(t) = Vfinal · (1 − e−t/RC) charging toward Vfinal from zero
VC(t) = V₀ · e−t/RC discharging from V₀ through R to ground — same curve, upside down

(Derivation sketch, for the calculus-inclined: the loop gives C·dVC/dt = (Vfinal − VC)/R, a first-order linear ODE whose solution is the exponential above. H&H would add: and if you remember only the shape and the time constant, you'll be fine.)

The time constant τ = RC

The product RC has units of seconds — ohms × farads = (volts/amp) × (coulombs/volt) = seconds. It is the characteristic time of the circuit: after one τ, the capacitor has covered 63% of the remaining distance to its target. After five, engineers stop watching.

Each τ knocks the remaining gap down by the same factor e ≈ 2.72. The curve never mathematically arrives, but 5τ is the traditional handshake between mathematics and engineering.

Capacitor combinations — resistors, mirrored

Capacitors combine by the opposite rules from resistors:

Cparallel = C₁ + C₂      Cseries = C₁C₂ / (C₁ + C₂) parallel adds; series follows the reciprocal rule — swap your resistor instincts

Why the reversal? In parallel, both capacitors see the same voltage and each stores its own charge — total charge (and hence total C = Q/V) adds. It's just more plate area. In series, the same charge is forced through the chain (the plate between them can only pass charge along, not create it), while the voltages add — same Q at more V means less capacitance. Two equal capacitors in series halve, exactly the way two equal resistors in parallel do.

A short capacitor bestiary

Real capacitors come in tribes, and picking the wrong tribe is a classic beginner bug:

TypeTypical rangeCharacter
Ceramic1 pF – 10 µF Tiny, cheap, fast, non-polarized. The default. The 100 nF decoupling workhorse.
Film1 nF – 10 µF Stable, precise, low leakage, non-polarized. Timing and audio love them.
Electrolytic1 µF – 10,000 µF Polarized! Big storage in small volume; leaky, sloppy tolerance (−20/+80% is common). The stripe marks −; observe the + terminal or it will vent — sometimes theatrically.

The 100 nF habit: every IC on a board gets a 100 nF ceramic from its power pin to ground, placed as close as physically possible. When a chip's logic switches, it gulps current in nanosecond spikes; the trace inductance back to the supply can't deliver that fast, so the local capacitor acts as a bucket of charge right at the pin. Distance matters because it's the wire between cap and pin that spoils the trick.

H&H rules of thumb
  • R in kΩ × C in µF = τ in ms. (Check: 1 kΩ × 1 µF = 10³ Ω × 10⁻⁶ F = 10⁻³ s = 1 ms ✓.) Equally handy: MΩ × µF = seconds, kΩ × nF = µs. Do this in your head, always.
  • 5τ ≈ fully charged (99.3%). 3τ (95%) when you're feeling brisk.
  • Decouple every chip with 100 nF ceramic, close to the pin. Not near the pin — at it.
  • Never reverse an electrolytic. Check the stripe twice; it only has to be wrong once.
  • Instant after switching: capacitor ≈ voltage source at its current voltage. Long after: open circuit. Sketch both limits before computing anything.
Plumbing analogy

Cut a water pipe and stretch a rubber membrane across it — a water balloon spliced into the line. No water ever crosses the membrane, yet the pipe seems to conduct:

  • Charging: push water in one side and the membrane bulges, shoving an equal flow out the other side. The more it stretches, the harder it pushes back — flow into it dies away exactly as the pressure difference (voltage) builds. That's I = C·dV/dt in rubber.
  • DC blocked, AC passed: a steady pressure just holds the membrane at a fixed bulge — zero flow. But sloshing passes straight through: each push-pull flexes the membrane and the far side sloshes in sympathy. Signal through, DC stopped.
  • τ = RC: a big, floppy membrane (large C) fed through a thin pipe (large R) takes a long time to stretch to its final bulge. Fat pipe or stiff little membrane — quick. The product of the two is the fill time.
  • Energy: the stretched rubber stores it, and gives it back when you let go — just like ½CV².

Where it fails: real membranes get stiffer as they stretch (capacitors are gloriously linear until the dielectric breaks down and the "membrane" ruptures for good), and the analogy has no natural picture for why a polarized electrolytic cares which way it's installed.

Square waves meet RC — a preview of filtering

Feed a square wave into R with C to ground (the "RC low-pass" you'll formalize in Module 05) and the output is the capacitor forever chasing a target that keeps jumping. Each half-period it relaxes exponentially toward the current input level; whether it ever gets there depends entirely on how the drive period T compares with τ. Once the waveform settles into a steady rhythm, the top and bottom levels it bounces between have a tidy closed form — for a square wave swinging 0 to 1:

Vhigh = 1 / (1 + e−T/2τ)     Vlow = e−T/2τ / (1 + e−T/2τ) steady-state bounce levels; they're symmetric about ½, and the swing is tanh(T/4τ)

(Where from? Call a = e−T/2τ. Over the high half-period the output relaxes from Vlow toward 1, ending at Vhigh = 1 + (Vlow − 1)a; over the low half it decays to Vlow = Vhigh·a. Two equations, two unknowns; solve and the pair above falls out.)

Check yourself

Design bench

Pencil, paper, and the kΩ×µF=ms trick. Check the box when you've done it and verified against the solution.

A microcontroller's reset pin (a high-impedance input, ~1 MΩ or better) must be held low for roughly 100 ms after power appears, then drift high. Design the RC: pick R from the supply to the pin and C from the pin to ground. Then argue: why not 10 MΩ with 10 nF? Why not 100 Ω with 1000 µF? What limits each extreme?

Show solution

Target τ in the 100 ms neighborhood (the pin crosses its threshold somewhere around 0.7–1 τ, so τ ≈ 100 ms is the right order). By the rule of thumb, kΩ × µF = ms: 100 kΩ × 1 µF = 100 ms ✓. Equally valid: 1 MΩ × 100 nF. Both give the same τ — the product is what matters, the split is an engineering choice.

Why not huge R, tiny C (10 MΩ × 10 nF)? The charging current through 10 MΩ is under a microamp — comparable to the input's own leakage current and the capacitor's. Those stray currents form an unplanned divider with your R and the "final" voltage may never convincingly cross the threshold, or the delay drifts wildly with temperature and humidity. Rule: keep R well below the input impedance and well above nothing — 10 k–1 M is the comfortable decade range here.

Why not tiny R, huge C (100 Ω × 1000 µF)? A 1000 µF electrolytic is physically large, costly, leaky, and sloppy (−20/+80% tolerance makes "100 ms" a fiction) — and at power-down it dumps its charge back through whatever path it finds. 100 kΩ with a small, tight film or ceramic part is cheaper and better in every way.

A pushbutton's contacts mechanically bounce for about 2 ms after a press. You're feeding a logic input with threshold Vth = 0.5·Vcc. Choose an RC so the input can't cross the threshold during the bounce storm, but a real press registers within 50 ms. (Useful fact: reaching 50% of the way takes t = τ·ln 2 ≈ 0.69τ.)

Show solution

The threshold math. Charging from 0 toward Vcc: V(t) = Vcc(1 − e−t/τ). Setting V = 0.5·Vcc gives e−t/τ = ½, i.e. t₅₀ = τ·ln 2 ≈ 0.693τ.

Bracket τ. Registration deadline: t₅₀ ≤ 50 ms → τ ≤ 50/0.693 ≈ 72 ms. Bounce immunity: even in the worst case (contact solidly closed for the whole 2 ms burst) the cap must stay under 50%, so we want t₅₀ comfortably beyond 2 ms — a 3–5× margin, say t₅₀ ≳ 7 ms → τ ≳ 10 ms.

Pick the middle: τ = 10 ms, e.g. 10 kΩ × 1 µF (kΩ×µF=ms again). Check both ends: after 2 ms of solid contact the cap reaches 1 − e−0.2 = 18% — far below threshold, and bounce gaps let it sag back besides. A real press crosses 50% at 0.693 × 10 = 6.9 ms — imperceptible, and well inside the 50 ms budget. (Purists add a Schmitt-trigger input so the slow ramp can't dither the logic — Module 10's problem.)

An audio amplifier input has 10 kΩ input impedance, and you must couple a signal into it while blocking the DC it rides on. The series capacitor plus the input impedance form a high-pass filter with corner frequency f = 1/(2πRC) (Module 05 will earn this formula; today, use it). You need the corner below 20 Hz so the deepest bass passes untouched. Find the minimum C and pick a standard value.

Show solution

Solve for C: C = 1/(2πRf) = 1/(2π × 10⁴ × 20) ≈ 0.8 µF (0.796 µF). Any larger C pushes the corner lower — good — so round up to the standard value: 1 µF.

Check: f = 1/(2π × 10 kΩ × 1 µF) ≈ 16 Hz — below 20 Hz, done. A film or ceramic part is fine here; if you reached for an electrolytic (say the next size up, 10 µF, corner 1.6 Hz), remember the polarity: + toward whichever side sits at the higher DC voltage.

The H&H reflex worth keeping: coupling caps are sized against the load's input impedance, and "bigger C" only costs money and size — the corner just slides lower.
