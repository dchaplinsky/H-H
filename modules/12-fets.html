FETs: JFETs & MOSFETs

The other transistor — controlled by voltage instead of current, drawing essentially nothing from whatever drives it. Thresholds, square laws, and the gate you must never leave floating. AoE 3rd ed. §3.1–3.2.

Why this matters

Part II has so far been the bipolar transistor's show: a current into the base commands a bigger current at the collector. The field-effect transistor plays the same role — a small terminal commanding a big current — with one deep difference: the control input is a voltage, and it draws essentially no current at all. Gate currents are picoamps for a JFET and femtoamps-to-nanoamps for a MOSFET, against the microamps a BJT's base demands. That one property is why your CPU contains billions of MOSFETs: a gate that draws no static current burns no static power, so you can afford billions of them switching only when asked. And at the other end of the scale, a power MOSFET will switch tens of amps while its gate sips microwatts of drive — the reason a bare microcontroller pin can run a motor, a heater, or a string of lamps through a single three-legged part. If Module 08's mantra was "the base is a current port," this module's is: the gate is a capacitor. Everything good and everything dangerous about FETs follows from that.

Physics background — control by field, not by current

The MOSFET's name is its cross-section: Metal (the gate electrode), Oxide (a glass insulation layer, mere nanometres thick), and Semiconductor (the silicon underneath, between two n-type wells called source and drain). The gate is literally one plate of a capacitor, with the channel region as the other plate. Put positive charge on the gate and its electric field reaches through the oxide and invites electrons to gather at the silicon surface — turning a thin sheet of normally-nonconducting p-type material into an n-type conducting channel from source to drain. No charge crosses the oxide; the field alone does the recruiting. That's the whole trick: a field, not a current, creates the channel — hence "field-effect." A device that needs gate voltage to create its channel is called enhancement mode (off at zero gate voltage — the overwhelmingly common kind); one built with a channel already present, which gate voltage can enrich or deplete, is depletion mode (on at zero — rare in MOSFETs, standard in JFETs).

The JFET gets the same result with older machinery: its channel is a real, physically doped conducting bar, and the gate is a reverse-biased PN junction alongside it (Module 06's depletion region, repurposed). The channel conducts at zero gate voltage — normally ON. Apply reverse gate voltage and the depletion region fattens, squeezing the channel like a thumb on a hose until, at the pinch-off voltage, it's fully choked. Because the gate junction stays reverse-biased, its "input current" is just diode leakage — picoamps. No oxide, so no oxide fragility; but also no enhancement mode, and the gate must never be driven a diode drop forward or it becomes, well, a diode.

The n-channel enhancement MOSFET — the workhorse

One FET flavor dominates practice so thoroughly that we'll learn it first and treat the rest as variations: the n-channel enhancement MOSFET. Its rules:

Below threshold: nothing. With the gate-to-source voltage VGS below a threshold Vth — typically 1–4 V, set at manufacture — there is no channel and no drain current worth mentioning. The device is an open switch.

Above threshold: a channel forms, and the drain current grows with the overdrive VGS − Vth:

ID ≈ k (VGS − Vth)² the square law — roughly quadratic in overdrive, unlike the BJT's exponential in VBE

Note the temperament change: the BJT's exponential meant 60 mV per decade; the MOSFET's square law is far gentler — doubling the overdrive quadruples the current. That makes FETs more forgiving to bias and less spectacular as amplifiers (lower transconductance at the same current), a trade we'll revisit below.

Two regions above threshold. The name of the game is comparing VDS with the overdrive:

  • Linear / triode region (VDS < VGS − Vth): the channel exists along its whole length and the device behaves like a voltage-controlled resistor — small VDS, current roughly proportional to it. This resistance is the famous RDS(on), and it's where every switching FET should live when "on." Full model: ID = k[2(VGS−Vth)VDS − VDS²].
  • Saturation region (VDS > VGS − Vth): the drain end of the channel pinches off, current stops caring about VDS, and the device behaves like a current source set by the square law. This is amplifier territory — the FET analog of the BJT's active region. (Confusingly, "saturation" means opposite things for BJTs and FETs. Blame history, learn both.)

The two expressions meet exactly at the boundary VDS = VGS − Vth — plug it into the triode formula and out falls k(VGS−Vth)², the saturation value. One smooth surface, two useful caricatures.

Plumbing analogy — the foot on the hose

A MOSFET is a garden hose with your foot resting on it. Press down and the flow chokes; ease off and it runs free. The crucial part of the picture: no water flows through your foot. Your foot does no flow-work at all — it just imposes a squeeze, exactly as the gate imposes a field without passing any current. A BJT, by contrast, is a valve you must pay a continuous trickle of water to hold open. (For the enhancement MOSFET, invert the default: the hose is squeezed shut until the "foot" — the gate field — opens it.)

The JFET is a hose that runs full-bore until you squeeze it — normally on, pinched off by effort, which is precisely the depletion region strangling the channel under reverse gate bias.

Where the analogy fails: feet don't have thresholds — a real gate does nothing at all until Vth, then follows a quadratic law no foot has ever obeyed. And the picture hides the gate's one running cost: the gate is a capacitor, and moving it fast means squirting real pulses of charge in and out, nanocoulombs at a time. DC-free is not charge-free — hold that thought for the switching-loss section.

Neither device wins; they split the trophies. Knowing which column matters for your circuit is most of the decision:

Gate care and feeding

The gate is a capacitor. Say it three times, because it has three consequences:

1. Zero DC current, real switching charge. Holding a power MOSFET on costs nothing, but its gate capacitance stores a total charge Qg of tens of nanocoulombs. To switch in 100 ns you must move that charge in 100 ns — I = Q/t = 20 nC / 100 ns = 0.2 A of gate current, briefly. Fast switching needs a driver that can source and sink real current pulses; DC-free does not mean effort-free.

2. A floating gate is a random-number generator. Disconnect a gate and its capacitor holds whatever charge it last had, drifting with leakage and picking up volts from any nearby field — your hand, the mains wiring, the drain node itself. The FET wanders between off, on, and the smoky in-between. Always give the gate a DC path: a 100 k pull-down (or pull-up, for a p-channel) guarantees the switch is off when the driving pin is absent, tri-stated, or still booting.

3. The oxide is nanometres thick. A static zap that a BJT wouldn't notice punches a permanent hole in a MOSFET gate. Ground yourself before handling bare parts, and touch the package tab rather than the pins.

And one datasheet trap that earns its own widget: logic-level versus standard gates. Vth is where the channel barely begins — the datasheet defines it at some token current like 250 µA. A FET driven just above threshold is not "on"; it's a high-value resistor. What you actually need is the gate voltage at which RDS(on) reaches its rated low value — and that's specified at a test condition, traditionally VGS = 10 V, or 4.5 V for "logic-level" parts. A "5 V" gate spec won't turn on properly from 3.3 V logic. Check both numbers: VGS(th) comfortably below your drive, and RDS(on) specified at (or below) the gate voltage you can actually supply.

RDS(on) and the price of switching

A fully-on MOSFET is a resistor, so Module 01's power law is the whole thermal story of a FET switch:

Pconduction = ID² · RDS(on) a 10 mΩ part carrying 5 A dissipates 0.25 W — no heatsink, no drama

This is the FET's great gift to power switching: RDS(on) comes in single-digit milliohms, so the on-state loss can be made almost arbitrarily small by buying a bigger die — no fixed 0.2 V floor as with a saturated BJT. The corollary cuts the other way: at VGS barely above threshold the same part is ohms, not milliohms, and I²R does its arithmetic without sympathy.

One more term joins the bill when you PWM: switching loss. During each transition the FET briefly sits at half-on — volts across it and amps through it at once. Each edge costs a fixed blob of energy, so switching loss grows linearly with frequency: slow PWM, negligible; hundreds of kilohertz, it can rival conduction loss. Fast gate drive (shorter transitions) is the lever — which is why consequence 1 above insisted on charge pulses.

JFETs — and the rest of the family

The JFET inverts the enhancement MOSFET's habits: it is normally on, and you apply reverse gate voltage (negative VGS, for an n-channel) to squeeze the channel down, reaching zero at the pinch-off voltage. Its gate is a reverse-biased diode — picoamp leakage, but also a hard rule: never drive it a diode drop positive or the gate conducts. What JFETs lack in switching glamour they repay in analog virtue: no oxide means no oxide traps, so JFETs make superbly low-noise, high-impedance front ends — condenser-microphone preamps, pH meters, the input stage of many fine op-amps — and their resistor-like triode region makes clean small-signal analog switches (Module 13's subject).

For completeness: depletion-mode MOSFETs exist — a MOSFET built with a channel already in place, normally on like a JFET but with an insulated gate. They're rare, prized for niche jobs like current limiters and start-up circuits in high-voltage supplies. If a schematic symbol seems to say "MOSFET that's on at zero volts," believe it — but expect to special-order the part.

H&H rules of thumb
  • Check VGS(th) at YOUR drive voltage — and check the test condition on the RDS(on) spec (10 V? 4.5 V?). If the datasheet measured at a gate voltage you can't supply, its resistance number isn't yours.
  • Never float a gate. 100 k to the off rail costs nothing and ends a whole category of mystery behavior.
  • Touch the tab, not the pins. The gate oxide is nanometres of glass; static electricity is its natural predator.
  • P = I²·RDS(on): a 10 mΩ part at 5 A dissipates 0.25 W — fine, bare part. The same part "on" at a gate voltage barely above threshold is a multi-ohm resistor and burns. Full enhancement or nothing.
  • Gate charge, not gate current, sets switching speed: Igate = Qg/t during the edges. Budget driver current if you PWM fast.

Design bench

Switch a 2 A, 12 V heater from a 3.3 V microcontroller output (low-side n-channel switch). Write the requirements the FET must meet, choose the supporting resistors, and compute the conduction loss for a part with RDS(on) = 30 mΩ. Does it need a heatsink?

Show solution

The part: a logic-level n-channel MOSFET with (1) VGS(th) ≤ 1.5 V, so 3.3 V clears it with ~2 V margin (recall the widget's verdict bands); (2) RDS(on) specified at VGS = 2.5 V — a spec at 2.5 V guarantees full enhancement anywhere at or above it, so your 3.3 V rail qualifies. A spec quoted only at 4.5 V is a gamble, and one only at 10 V is a refusal; (3) VDS rating ≥ 20–30 V (headroom over 12 V) and ID rating comfortably over 2 A.

The supporting cast: ~100 Ω in series with the gate — it tames ringing between the pin and the gate capacitance and limits the charging-current spike the microcontroller pin must deliver — plus 100 kΩ from gate to ground, so the heater is guaranteed off while the micro is resetting, rebooting, or unplugged. The pull-down loses the argument with the pin (100 k versus 100 Ω) whenever the pin actually drives, which is exactly the arrangement you want.

The loss: P = I²·RDS(on) = 2² × 0.030 = 0.12 W. A bare SOT-23 or DPAK sheds that with no heatsink and barely a rise in temperature. Compare a BJT doing the same job: 0.2 V × 2 A = 0.4 W plus ~20 mA of continuous base drive from the pin — more heat and a hundred times the control power.

Two field reports, one part number. Board A: a MOSFET switching a solenoid worked on the bench for an hour, then failed shorted; the schematic shows the solenoid coil straight from +12 V to the drain, nothing else. Board B: same FET switching a fan; on some units the fan spins at half speed with the FET too hot to touch — before the microcontroller firmware has even started. The gate trace runs to the micro's pin and to nothing else. Diagnose both.

Show solution

Board A: inductive kick, no flyback path. Module 04's iron law: an inductor's current cannot stop instantly. Each turn-off, the solenoid yanks the drain voltage skyward until something conducts — and that something is the FET's own drain-source junction breaking down in avalanche, tens of volts above its rating, swallowing the coil's stored energy every single cycle. Small FETs tolerate occasional avalanche; repetitive energy dumps cook the die until it fails (MOSFETs usually fail shorted, hence the always-on solenoid found in the wreckage). Fix: a flyback diode across the coil — Module 06's bodyguard reflex — or a properly avalanche-rated FET chosen on purpose, with the datasheet's repetitive-energy numbers checked.

Board B: a floating gate. Before firmware boots, the micro's pin is a high-impedance input — the gate is connected to nothing. Its capacitor holds whatever charge assembly and handling left on it; on some units that sits near threshold, and the FET half-turns-on: a few ohms in series with the fan. Half speed, and P = I²R with R in the ohms instead of milliohms — hence the burnt fingers. Fix: the 100 k gate pull-down from Task 1. One resistor, and the "random FET behavior before boot" bug class simply ceases to exist.

By analogy with the BJT emitter follower (Module 09): source follower — drain to the positive rail, load resistor from source to ground, output at the source, so Vout = Vin − VGS. Is this a good follower? Discuss the offset, and name the applications where a FET follower beats a bipolar one anyway.

Show solution

The offset is bigger and vaguer than the BJT's. An emitter follower sits a dependable ~0.6 V below its input — the exponential pins VBE to within tens of millivolts regardless of current. The FET's VGS is Vth plus the overdrive: with Vth anywhere from 1 to 4 V by type — scattering part-to-part and drifting with temperature — plus a square-law overdrive that moves with load current (the gentle square law means the gate voltage must swing more than a VBE would to cover the same current range), the offset is larger, poorly predictable, and signal-dependent. That last part is nonlinearity, not just offset. Verdict: as a precision level-shifter, it's poor; the classic fixes are a current-source load (fixed ID → fixed overdrive, Module 11's trick) or feedback around the whole stage.

Where it wins anyway: the input impedance is essentially infinite. The BJT follower's base still draws Iload/β — µA that will flatten a gigaohm source. The FET's gate draws picoamps or less. So FET followers own the electrometer front ends: pH probes (GΩ source impedance), condenser and electret microphones, photodiodes in photovoltaic mode, ionization chambers — any source so frail that merely being observed by a base current would ruin it. There, a volt or two of vague offset is a bargain, and the follower exists precisely to convert a gigaohm world down to something a BJT stage can meet politely.
