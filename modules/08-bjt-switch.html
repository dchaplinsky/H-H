The BJT as a Switch

A small current steering a big one — the atom of everything active. Your first transistor circuit, and still the one you'll build most often. AoE 3rd ed. §2.1–2.2.

Why this matters

Everything in Part I was passive: resistors, capacitors and diodes can shape, store and steer signals, but they can only ever lose. The transistor is the part that made electronics active — a device in which a small current controls a big one. That single trick is the atom of amplification, and every op-amp, radio and processor is that atom repeated in clever arrangements. But before you amplify anything, you meet the transistor's simplest and most-used incarnation: the switch. A microcontroller pin that can wiggle a few milliamps flips a transistor, and the transistor sinks the hundred milliamps the LED strip, relay coil or motor actually wants. Every logic gate ever built is transistor switches; every "drive this thing from that pin" problem you will ever face starts here. Learn the recipe in this module and you'll use it for the rest of your life, mostly without thinking.

Physics background — the NPN sandwich

Take the PN junction from Module 06 and add one more layer: an NPN transistor is a sliver of p-type silicon (the base) sandwiched between two n-type regions (emitter and collector). That's two PN junctions back to back — and back-to-back diodes shouldn't conduct at all. The magic is that the base layer is made absurdly thin.

Forward-bias the base–emitter junction — it behaves exactly like the diode you already know, turning on at ~0.6 V — and the emitter injects a flood of electrons into the base. In a fat base they'd all recombine with holes and exit through the base lead, and you'd have an ordinary diode. But the base is so thin that almost every injected electron diffuses across it before finding a hole, falls into the reverse-biased base–collector junction's field, and is swept out through the collector. Only a small fraction — one in a hundred or so — recombines and shows up as base current. Flip that ratio around and you get the headline result: IC ≈ β·IB, with β (also written hFE) typically 50 to 300.

And here is the number-one thing H&H want you to absorb about β: it is not a reliable number. It varies part to part (a factor of several within one part number), with temperature, and with collector current. Any circuit whose behavior depends on the exact value of β is a bad circuit. Everything in Part II is, one way or another, a technique for not caring what β is.

H&H's mental model, worth adopting: "transistor man" — a little homunculus inside the package who watches the base current and continuously adjusts a collector rheostat to keep IC = β·IB… if he can. When the external circuit can't supply that much collector current, he simply opens his rheostat all the way and gives you everything available. That "all the way open" state is where the switch lives.

The three terminals & the rules

For switching (and most everything else), three rules carry you:

  • Rule 1 — the base–emitter junction is a diode. When the transistor conducts, VBE ≈ 0.6 V (0.7 V when driven hard), and like any diode it will pass unlimited current if nothing limits it. Below ~0.6 V the transistor is off.
  • Rule 2 — in the active region, IC = β·IB. The collector current is a β-times copy of the base current, provided the collector circuit can deliver it. (Note what the collector voltage doesn't appear in this rule at all — the collector behaves like a current sink, not a resistor.)
  • Rule 3 — saturation. Keep raising IB and eventually β·IB exceeds what the load can supply: the collector "runs out of voltage." The transistor then drops as little as it can — VCE(sat) ≈ 0.1–0.3 V — and the load, not β, sets the current. This is the fully-on switch state.
VBE(on) ≈ 0.6 V     IC = β·IB (active)     VCE(sat) ≈ 0.1–0.3 V (saturated) β ~ 50–300, and don't you dare design as if you knew which

So a switching transistor has three states: off (no base current, no collector current, full supply voltage across C–E), active (the linear in-between — useful for amplifiers, a heat-generating failure for switches), and saturated (fully on, ~0.2 V across it, load current set by the load). A good switch design sprints through the middle state and lives at the ends.

Plumbing analogy — the pilot valve

The base current is a small hand on a big valve lever. Think of a pilot valve — or power steering: a trickle of flow through the thin pilot line opens the main gate, and a torrent goes through. The trickle doesn't become the torrent; it controls it, and the ratio of torrent to trickle is β. Saturation is the valve fully open: once the gate is against its stop, pushing the lever harder does nothing more — the flow is now set by the pipes, not the valve. That's exactly the switch's happy state: shove in more base current than strictly needed, and the excess simply guarantees the gate is hard against the stop.

Where the analogy fails: a real transistor is a continuous, current-controlled device, not a binary open/shut gate — the whole active region between off and saturated is real, and amplifiers live there. The analogy also hides the 0.6 V base–emitter threshold (the lever has a preload spring the water picture doesn't show), and it says nothing about storage time: a hard-saturated transistor has extra charge parked in its base, and it turns off noticeably slower than it turned on — no mechanical valve does that.

The switch recipe

Here is the circuit, and it barely ever changes:

  • Load in the collector. The LED (with its own series resistor), relay coil or motor sits between the positive supply and the collector.
  • Emitter straight to ground. No resistor, no cleverness — grounding the emitter is what makes the base so easy to drive (0.6 V above ground, always).
  • Drive the base through RB. The base–emitter junction is a diode; the resistor converts your logic voltage into a defined base current.
  • Design for saturation. This is the rule that makes the circuit a switch: choose IB ≈ IC/10 — a "forced beta" of 10 — even though the datasheet promises β of 100 or more. You are deliberately overdriving the base by 5–30×, so that the weakest transistor of the batch, on the coldest day, still slams fully on. Never trust datasheet β; it's a minimum-to-maximum rumor, and your overdrive is the margin that makes it irrelevant.

Why so obsessed with saturation? Power. The transistor dissipates P = VCE·IC. Saturated, that's ~0.2 V × the load current — milliwatts. Stuck halfway in the active region, VCE can be volts, and the same load current makes real heat in a part with no heatsink. A saturated switch is cool because it's fully on; a half-on switch is a soldering iron.

Sizing RB

One line of arithmetic. The base sits at 0.6 V (Rule 1), so the resistor sees whatever's left of the drive voltage:

RB = (Vdrive − 0.6 V) / IB     with IB = IC / 10 round RB down to the next standard value — more base drive is the safe direction

Worked example. A 5 V logic output must switch a 100 mA load. Forced beta of 10 says IB = 100 mA / 10 = 10 mA. Then RB = (5 − 0.6) V / 10 mA = 4.4 V / 10 mA = 440 Ω. Nearest standard value: 470 Ω. Verify — the habit that separates designs from guesses: IB = 4.4 V / 470 Ω = 9.4 mA, a forced beta of 10.7. Even a sorry β = 50 specimen would offer β·IB ≈ 470 mA — nearly five times what the load allows — so it saturates with room to spare. (470 Ω is a hair above the computed 440, which is why you check; here the margin is so thick it doesn't matter. When it's thin, round down.)

Loads — what hangs off the collector

LED

The gentlest load. The LED still needs its own series resistor (Module 01 arithmetic, with one refinement: the resistor now sees supply − LED drop − VCE(sat)). The transistor adds only ~0.2 V to the loop — which is the whole charm: a 3.3 V microcontroller pin can switch LEDs on a 12 V rail it could never touch directly.

Relay coil

A relay coil is an inductor, and Module 04 told you what inductors do when you interrupt their current: generate whatever voltage it takes to keep it flowing. Switch off 70 mA of coil current abruptly and the collector flies to hundreds of volts — once. The fix is one flyback diode across the coil, cathode to the supply: reverse-biased and invisible while the relay is on, it gives the coil current a one-way exit loop at turn-off, clamping the collector to a diode drop above the rail. No inductive load without its flyback diode, ever. The schematic in the bench above shows it wired in.

Small motor

A DC motor is an inductor with ambitions (and a generator, and a source of brush noise), so it gets the same flyback diode — plus skepticism about its current: a motor draws several times its running current at start-up and when stalled. Size the switch for stall current, not the label on the box.

Low-side, high-side, and the PNP mirror image

Everything above is low-side switching: the load lives between supply and collector, and the transistor connects its bottom end to ground. That's the easy, default case — the emitter is grounded, so "on" is just 0.6 V on the base, no matter what the load's supply is. But sometimes the load must stay grounded and you have to break the top of the loop — high-side switching. An NPN is awkward there: with the load under its emitter, the base must ride ~0.6 V above the load voltage, which your logic pin can't reach.

Enter the PNP — the NPN's mirror image. Same sandwich with the doping swapped, so every arrow, current and polarity reverses: the emitter goes to the positive supply, current flows emitter→collector, and it turns on when the base is pulled ~0.6 V below the emitter. Perfect for the high side: emitter to +V, load from collector to ground, and pulling the base low (through RB) switches it on. Everything you've learned applies upside-down — same forced-beta rule, same saturation, same arithmetic with the signs flipped. (One trap for later: if the load's supply is higher than your logic high, a lone PNP never turns fully off — the standard fix is a small NPN driving the PNP's base. Filed under "you'll meet it again.")

H&H rules of thumb
  • Saturate with IB = IC/10. Forced beta of 10; the datasheet's β of 100+ is your margin, never your design value.
  • Numbers to carry: VBE ≈ 0.6 V, VCE(sat) ≈ 0.2 V.
  • Never leave a base floating. An open base picks up whatever charge and interference wander by; if "off" matters, tie the base down (drive it low, or add a base–emitter resistor).
  • β is a rumor, not a spec — 50 to 300 for the same part number. Circuits that depend on its exact value are broken by design.
  • Power in the switch: P = VCE(sat)·IC — a couple of hundred millivolts times the load current, i.e. almost nothing. That's the whole point of saturating. The active region, where VCE is volts, is where switches go to overheat.
  • Inductive load (relay, motor, solenoid) → flyback diode. No exceptions (Module 04).

Check yourself

Design bench

The 0.6 V / β·IB / 0.2 V toolkit and a calculator are all you need. Check the box once you've worked each one and compared against the solution.

A microcontroller pin (3.3 V logic) must switch a 12 V LED strip that draws 30 mA. Draw the circuit, choose RB, and check the transistor's dissipation and the load on the microcontroller pin.

Show solution

Low-side NPN: strip between +12 V and the collector, emitter to ground, pin → RB → base. The pin never sees 12 V — that's the trick. Note the two supplies must share a ground, or the base current has no return path.

Forced beta of 10: IB = 30 mA / 10 = 3 mA. The base sits at 0.6 V, so RB = (3.3 − 0.6) V / 3 mA = 2.7 V / 3 mA = 900 Ω → round down to standard 820 Ω. Verify: IB = 2.7 / 820 = 3.3 mA, forced beta 9.1 — even a β = 50 part offers ~165 mA, 5× the load. Saturated.

Checks: transistor power = VCE(sat)·IC ≈ 0.2 V × 30 mA = 6 mW — stone cold, any small NPN (2N3904, BC547) shrugs. Pin load: 3.3 mA against a typical 20 mA pin rating — comfortable. The strip has its own internal resistors, so no extra series R needed here.

A 5 V relay whose coil draws 72 mA is to be driven from a 5 V logic output. Choose RB, pick the flyback diode, and narrate exactly what happens in the first moments after turn-off.

Show solution

IB = 72 mA / 10 = 7.2 mA; RB = (5 − 0.6) / 7.2 mA = 4.4 V / 7.2 mA ≈ 611 Ω → round down to 560 Ω. Verify: IB = 4.4 / 560 = 7.9 mA, forced beta 9.2. On-state dissipation ≈ 0.2 V × 72 mA ≈ 14 mW. Fine.

Flyback diode: a 1N4148 (200 mA rating) or 1N4001 across the coil, cathode to +5 V. The diode must carry the coil's full 72 mA at the instant of turn-off — the 1N4148 has 3× margin; nothing exotic required.

Turn-off, frame by frame: the base drive vanishes and the transistor stops conducting (after a whisker of storage-time delay — it was saturated). The coil's 72 mA cannot stop instantly (Module 04), so the collector voltage flies upward… and at 5 + 0.6 = 5.6 V the flyback diode conducts. The current now circulates coil→diode→coil, decaying with time constant L/R over a few milliseconds while the relay armature drops out. Without the diode, the collector would spike to whatever voltage breaks the transistor first — usually a one-experiment lesson.

A colleague's board switches a small 20 mA vibration motor from 5 V logic, low-side NPN, RB = 47 kΩ. With the transistor from the first prototype it works. After a rebuild with a same-part-number transistor from a new reel, the motor barely twitches and the transistor runs warm. "Same circuit, same part — must be a bad transistor," they say. Diagnose and fix.

Show solution

Run the numbers first: IB = (5 − 0.6) / 47 kΩ = 94 µA. To saturate 20 mA the transistor needs β > 20 mA / 94 µA ≈ 214. The datasheet's range is 100–300: the first transistor won the β lottery (a ~300 specimen → up to ~28 mA available, just barely saturates); the new one is a perfectly in-spec β ≈ 100 part offering only β·IB ≈ 9 mA. It's parked in the active region: the motor gets half its current, and the transistor drops several volts at several milliamps — hence warm. Neither transistor is bad; the design was a bet on β, and it finally lost.

Fix: design for forced beta of 10. IB = 2 mA → RB = 4.4 V / 2 mA = 2.2 kΩ (a standard value, no rounding needed). Now even a β = 50 part offers 100 mA against a 20 mA load — every transistor on the reel saturates. And while you're in there: a motor is an inductive load — make sure the flyback diode is actually fitted. Moral, verbatim from the rules panel: β is a rumor, not a spec.
