The Op-Amp Toolbox

Five circuits built from one trick: pour currents into the virtual ground and let the feedback element decide what mathematics comes out. AoE 3rd ed. §4.3.

Why this matters

Module 14's golden rules are a grammar: the inputs draw no current, and feedback forces them equal. This module is the phrasebook — the five or six op-amp sentences that appear on every analog schematic ever drawn. A mixing console is summing amplifiers. A strain-gauge readout is a difference amplifier. A function generator's triangle wave comes from an integrator; a spectrometer's photodiode feeds a transimpedance amplifier; and half of all precision instrumentation hides a rectifier with the diode tucked inside a feedback loop. Learn to recognize these five shapes and whole pages of schematics collapse into readable phrases: "mix, subtract, accumulate, differentiate, convert." Design-wise they're even better — each one is two or three components hung on an op-amp, with the math printed right on the resistor values.

Physics background

One idea powers everything in this module: the virtual ground is a current summing node. In the inverting configuration the feedback pins the inverting input at 0 V (rule 2), and the input itself swallows nothing (rule 1). So every current that arrives at that node — through an input resistor, out of a photodiode, from anywhere — has exactly one place to go: through the feedback element. The output voltage is simply whatever value makes that happen. That's the whole theory. The op-amp does no arithmetic; it just holds a node at zero and pays whatever the feedback element charges for carrying the current away.

Which means the feedback element is the mathematical operation. Make it a resistor and the output is proportional to the arriving current — multiplication: Vout = −I·Rf. Make it a capacitor and the arriving current piles up as stored charge (Module 03: I = C·dV/dt, so V = (1/C)∫I dt) — the output becomes the running total of everything that ever flowed in: integration. Swap the resistor and capacitor and you get the derivative instead. One node, one accounting rule, a whole toolbox of operations.

The summing amplifier — a mixer with math

Take Module 14's inverting amplifier and hang more input resistors onto the same virtual ground. Each source Vn pushes a current Vn/Rn into the node — and because the node sits at a rock-solid 0 V, each source computes its current knowing nothing about the others. The feedback resistor must carry the sum, so:

Vout = −Rf (V₁/R₁ + V₂/R₂ + V₃/R₃ + …) each channel's gain is −Rf/Rn — an independent knob per input

This is the audio engineer's mixer: every microphone gets its own input resistor (its own fader, really), and the virtual ground is the mixing bus. The magic is the isolation — turning channel 2's gain up doesn't change channel 1's gain, because both dump into a node whose voltage never moves. Try that with a passive resistor network and every input loads every other (Module 02's divider headaches, multiplied). The virtual ground makes crosstalk mathematically zero.

The difference amplifier — subtract, then amplify

Four resistors and one op-amp: V₁ enters through R₁ into the inverting input with R₂ as feedback; V₂ enters through an identical R₁ into the non-inverting input, which is held by a matching R₂ divider to ground. Grind through the golden rules and the result is beautifully clean:

Vout = (R₂/R₁)(V₂ − V₁) true only when both R₂/R₁ ratios match — matching is the whole game

This circuit amplifies the difference between two signals while (ideally) ignoring whatever voltage they share — the common-mode signal, like the hum both wires of a long sensor cable pick up together. But "ideally" leans entirely on resistor matching: if the two R₂/R₁ ratios disagree, a slice of the common-mode voltage leaks through as if it were signal. With ordinary 1% resistors the worst-case CMRR of a unity-gain difference amp is about 34 dB — Module 11's long-tailed pair would blush. That's why the industrial-strength version exists: the instrumentation amplifier, three op-amps and a laser-trimmed matched resistor network on one chip, delivering 100+ dB of CMRR and a gain set by a single external resistor. When a difference measurement matters, buy the matching.

The integrator — a capacitor keeps the books

Replace Rf with a capacitor. Input current Vin/R still arrives at the virtual ground and must still leave through the feedback — but now "leaving" means accumulating as charge on C, and the output must ramp to keep the node pinned:

Vout = −(1/RC) ∫ Vin dt the output is the running total of the input's past — a memory made of charge

Feed it a constant voltage and the output is a ramp (slope −Vin/RC). Feed it a square wave and out comes a triangle wave — the integrator's signature, and the heart of every function generator. But the memory is also the menace: an integrator integrates everything, including the op-amp's own input offset voltage and bias current. Any DC at the input, however microscopic, ramps the output relentlessly toward a supply rail — a 1 mV offset into RC = 1 ms drifts at a full volt per second. Every practical integrator therefore needs a DC escape path: a large resistor across C (rolling the DC gain down from "infinite" to −Rbig/R), or a reset switch that periodically empties the bucket. No escape path, no working circuit — this is the classic first-op-amp-project failure.

The differentiator — the mirror image, with teeth

Swap the components: C in series with the input, R as feedback. Now the input current is C·dVin/dt (Module 03's law again), and the feedback resistor converts it to:

Vout = −RC · dVin/dt output proportional to how fast the input is changing

Feed it a triangle wave and out comes a square wave — constant slopes become constant levels. Feed it a square wave and it tries to output the derivative of a vertical edge: an impulse, slamming into the rails. And therein lies the problem: a differentiator's gain is ωRC, rising without limit as frequency rises. Noise lives at high frequency; a differentiator is a noise amplifier by construction, and the fast phase shift also invites oscillation. The standard taming is a small resistor in series with C, which flattens the gain above some frequency and turns the circuit into "differentiator below, inverting amp above." H&H's honest advice: differentiators are trouble — tame them, or restructure the problem so you can integrate instead.

The transimpedance amplifier — current in, voltage out

Some sources speak current, not voltage: photodiodes, photomultipliers, DACs with current outputs. Feed the current straight into the virtual ground — no input resistor at all — and the feedback resistor does the conversion:

Vout = −Iin · Rf a current-to-voltage converter; Rf is the "gain" in volts per amp

The canonical customer is the photodiode front end — light in, photocurrent out, microamps per milliwatt. Why not just push the photocurrent through a plain resistor and read the voltage? Because then the diode's own voltage would swing as the light changes, and that ruins two things at once: the diode's junction capacitance (Module 06) must charge and discharge through the resistor, murdering the speed; and a biased photodiode is measurably nonlinear. The transimpedance amp holds the photodiode at exactly 0 V forever — no capacitance ever charges, no bias ever varies, and the diode operates in its most linear mode, delivering pure current into a node that never argues. Same current, same resistor, vastly better measurement: the virtual ground is doing all the work.

The precision rectifier — feedback fixes even diodes

Module 06 left a scar: a diode rectifier loses 0.6 V, so millivolt-scale signals simply vanish. The op-amp cure is to put the diode inside the feedback loop: op-amp output → diode → circuit output, with feedback taken after the diode. The golden rules force the final output to track the input, so the op-amp simply swings its own output 0.6 V higher than needed, absorbing the diode drop as if it weren't there. The result rectifies signals of a few millivolts as crisply as volts — an "ideal diode" conjured from an ordinary one. The transferable moral, one last time: anything placed inside a feedback loop has its errors divided by the loop gain — diode drops, output impedance, distortion, all of it.

H&H rules of thumb
  • Virtual ground = current accounting. To read any inverting-family circuit, sum the currents into the node and ask what output makes the feedback element carry them away. That one move analyzes everything in this module.
  • Summing gains are independent knobs. Each channel's gain is −Rf/Rn, full stop — the virtual ground isolates the inputs from each other completely.
  • Every practical integrator needs a DC escape path — a big resistor across C or a reset switch. Otherwise offset and bias current walk the output to a rail, always.
  • Differentiators are noise machines — gain rises with frequency forever. Tame with a series resistor, or redesign so you integrate instead.
  • Keep impedances roughly 1 kΩ–1 MΩ. Smaller loads the op-amp output and wastes current; larger invites bias-current errors, Johnson noise, and stray capacitance corners. (Transimpedance feedback resistors earn an exemption when the photocurrent is tiny.)
Funnel-and-pump analogy

Picture the summing node as a funnel whose water level must stay pinned exactly at the rim of a drain — the 0 V mark. Several streams pour in (the input currents, each set by its own source and pipe). At the drain sits a pump — the op-amp output — that must pump out precisely what arrives, moment by moment, to keep the level from budging. Watch the pump's effort and you've measured the total inflow: that's the summing amplifier. The streams never disturb each other because the level they pour into never changes — that's channel isolation.

Now plumb a bucket (a capacitor) into the drain line. The pump must now raise the bucket's contents to keep the funnel level pinned, and the bucket's fill records the total volume that has ever arrived — integration, as a water meter. Drill a pinhole in the bucket (the reset resistor) and old history slowly leaks away, which is exactly what keeps the bucket from eventually overflowing from a slow dribble no one noticed.

Where the analogy fails: real funnels overflow — the op-amp output hits its supply rails and the level-pinning fiction collapses. Real pumps have a maximum flow (output current limit) and a reaction lag when the inflow changes abruptly (slew rate — Module 18 makes this quantitative). Inside those limits, the accounting is exact.

Check yourself

Design bench

Pencil and paper; the only formulas you need are on this page. Check the box when you've done it and verified against the solution.

Build a summing amplifier for three line-level sources with channel gains of 1, 2, and 0.5 (magnitudes — the whole mix comes out inverted, which we'll accept; audio doesn't care about absolute polarity, and a second inverting stage could fix it later anyway). Choose Rf = 20 kΩ and find the three input resistors, then check them against the standard-value drawers.

Show solution

Each channel's gain magnitude is Rf/Rn, so Rn = Rf/gain:

Gain 1 → R₁ = 20 kΩ/1 = 20 kΩ (E24 standard — done). Gain 2 → R₂ = 20 kΩ/2 = 10 kΩ (every drawer on earth — done). Gain 0.5 → R₃ = 20 kΩ/0.5 = 40 kΩ — and here the drawer pushes back: 40 kΩ isn't E24. Nearest neighbors: 39 kΩ gives a gain of 20/39 = 0.513 (2.6% high — inaudible), or reach for the E96 drawer's 40.2 kΩ for 0.498. For audio, 39 kΩ and move on.

Sanity checks worth the habit: all values sit comfortably in the 1 kΩ–1 MΩ window, and each channel's gain came from its own resistor only — you set three gains with three independent knobs, courtesy of the virtual ground. A passive mixer could never do that.

You have a clean 1 kHz, ±5 V square wave and you want a ±2.5 V triangle wave. Design the integrator: derive the required RC, then pick standard R and C values and state the actual output amplitude. Don't forget the component every practical integrator needs.

Show solution

Derive the peak. During each half-period T/2 the input is a constant ±A, so the output ramps at A/RC and climbs A·(T/2)/RC in total — that's the peak-to-peak. The peak is half that: Vpk = A·T/(4RC). Solve for RC with A = 5 V, T = 1 ms, Vpk = 2.5 V: RC = 5 × 10⁻³/(4 × 2.5) = 0.5 ms.

Pick parts. R = 10 kΩ with C = 50 nF is exact but 50 nF isn't standard; 10 kΩ + 47 nF (RC = 0.47 ms) gives Vpk = 5 × 10⁻³/(4 × 4.7 × 10⁻⁴) ≈ 2.66 V — 6% big. Prefer the low side? 5.1 kΩ + 100 nF (RC = 0.51 ms) gives 2.45 V. Either is a fine answer; knowing which way you rounded is the real skill.

The mandatory extra: a DC escape path — say 1 MΩ across C. Without it, the square wave's tiniest asymmetry plus the op-amp's own offset integrate forever and the triangle rides a ramp into the rail. With it, DC gain is capped at −1 MΩ/10 kΩ = −100, and the 1 kHz triangle is untouched (the escape corner sits at 1/(2π × 1 MΩ × 47 nF) ≈ 3.4 Hz, far below 1 kHz).

A photodiode delivers 0–50 µA across the ambient-light range you care about, and your microcontroller's ADC accepts 0–3.3 V. Design the transimpedance stage: find the ideal Rf, pick a standard value, state the actual full-scale output — and deal with the sign.

Show solution

Size Rf. Full scale wants |Vout| = I·Rf: Rf = 3.3 V/50 µA = 66 kΩ. Round down so full sunlight can't overrun the ADC: the E24 drawer offers 62 kΩ, giving 50 µA × 62 kΩ = 3.1 V full scale — 94% of the ADC range used, 6% safety margin. (68 kΩ would hit 3.4 V and clip the brightest readings — rounding direction is a design decision, not arithmetic.)

The sign. Vout = −I·Rf = 0 to −3.1 V as drawn — useless to an ADC that wants positive. The clean fix costs nothing: flip the photodiode. Reverse its orientation and the photocurrent flows out of the summing node instead of in, so the output swings 0 to +3.1 V. (The op-amp must be able to swing to (near) 0 V and run from the ADC's supply — a rail-to-rail single-supply part; the alternative, a second inverting stage, spends a whole op-amp to fix a sign.) The photodiode itself still sits at 0 V either way — the virtual ground doesn't care which direction the current flows, and neither does the accounting.
