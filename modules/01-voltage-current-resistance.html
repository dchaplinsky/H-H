Voltage, Current & Ohm's Law

The three quantities every schematic is silently talking about — and the one-line law that connects them. AoE 3rd ed. §1.1–1.2.

Why this matters

Every circuit you will ever read or design is a story about voltage pushing current through things that resist it. Horowitz & Hill open the book with exactly this trio because the rest of electronics — transistors, op-amps, digital logic — is built from clever arrangements of the same three ideas. If you can glance at a schematic and estimate the currents and voltages at each node, you can read almost anything.

Physics background

Metals are full of electrons that are free to wander between the fixed atoms of the lattice. Normally they wander randomly and no net charge moves. Apply an electric field — by connecting a battery — and the whole electron cloud starts drifting. Surprisingly slowly, too: in a typical copper wire the drift velocity is well under a millimetre per second. The reason your lamp lights instantly is that the electromagnetic push propagates near the speed of light; the electrons everywhere in the loop start moving essentially at once, like water in an already-full hose.

Charge is measured in coulombs (C); one electron carries −1.6×10−19 C. Current is the rate of charge flow: 1 ampere = 1 coulomb/second. Voltage is energy per charge: 1 volt = 1 joule/coulomb — how much work the field does moving each coulomb between two points.

The three players

Voltage — the push

Voltage is always between two points. It is the work needed to move a unit of charge from one point to the other. Saying "the voltage at this node" is shorthand for "the voltage between this node and ground" — the reference point we agree to call 0 V. Symbol V (sometimes E), unit volts (V).

Current — the flow

Current flows through things. It is the rate at which charge streams past a point. Symbol I, unit amperes (A). By convention, current flows from + to − (even though the electrons physically drift the other way — a historical accident of Franklin's that harms nothing as long as you are consistent).

Resistance — the friction

Real materials oppose current. For a huge and useful class of materials ("ohmic" ones — carbon, metal films) the opposition is constant: double the push, get double the flow. That constant is resistance: symbol R, unit ohms (Ω).

V = I × R     I = V / R     R = V / I Ohm's law — memorize all three rearrangements until they're reflexes
Plumbing analogy

Think of a closed loop of water pipe with a pump:

  • Voltage ↔ pressure difference between two points in the pipe. The pump (battery) creates it. Pressure at a single point means nothing until you compare it with somewhere else — just like voltage.
  • Current ↔ flow rate — litres per second passing any cross-section. In a single loop it's the same everywhere, because water doesn't pile up. Neither does charge: what flows out of one end of a resistor flows in at the other.
  • Resistance ↔ a narrow section of pipe. Same pressure, thinner pipe → less flow. And forcing flow through the narrow bit dissipates energy as heat and turbulence — exactly what a resistor does.

Where the analogy fails (all analogies do): the wire is always "full of water" — you never wait for charge to arrive — and there's no gravity pulling charge "down".

Power — where the energy goes

Pushing current through a resistance converts electrical energy into heat, at a rate

P = V × I = I²R = V²/R watts = volts × amps; the I²R and V²/R forms follow by substituting Ohm's law

This matters practically: every real resistor has a power rating (¼ W is the common small part). Exceed it and the part cooks. Estimating P is a habit that H&H drill from page one — do it for every resistor you place.

Series and parallel — first contact

Two resistors in series carry the same current, so their voltages add — total resistance is the sum. Two in parallel see the same voltage, so their currents add — total resistance shrinks:

Rseries = R₁ + R₂      Rparallel = R₁R₂ / (R₁ + R₂) parallel is always smaller than the smallest member; equal pairs halve
H&H rules of thumb
  • A large resistor in series with a small one ≈ the large one. In parallel ≈ the small one.
  • Shortcut for parallel pairs: two equal resistors halve; 10k ∥ 10k = 5k.
  • To beat down a resistance by ~10%, parallel it with ten times its value.
  • Always ask "roughly what current flows here, and what power does that make?" — estimation beats calculation for reading schematics.

Check yourself

Design bench

Real tasks, pencil-and-paper (or head) only. Check the box when you've done it and verified against the solution.

You want to run an LED from a 5 V supply. The LED drops 2.0 V when lit and wants 10 mA. Choose a resistor value and its minimum power rating, then pick the nearest standard value.

Show solution

The resistor sees whatever the LED doesn't: 5 − 2 = 3 V. For 10 mA: R = 3 V / 10 mA = 300 Ω. Nearest standard (E24) value: 300 Ω exactly exists; 330 Ω is the more common drawer value and gives 9.1 mA — fine.

Power: P = I²R = (0.01)² × 300 = 30 mW. A ¼ W (250 mW) part has >8× margin. Habit to build: compute P even when you're sure it's tiny.

A 100 Ω, ¼ W resistor is connected straight across a 9 V battery. Is it happy? What's the largest supply voltage this part tolerates continuously?

Show solution

P = V²/R = 81/100 = 0.81 W — more than three times its ¼ W rating. It will get scorching hot and eventually fail (often drifting high in value first).

Maximum voltage: V = √(P·R) = √(0.25 × 100) = 5 V. In practice you'd derate — H&H suggest staying below ~50–70% of rated power for longevity.

Sketch this from the description, then analyze: a 12 V source feeds two resistors in series, 1 kΩ then 2 kΩ, returning to the source. Find the current, the voltage across each resistor, and the power delivered by the source. Then answer: which single fact made the whole analysis possible?

Show solution

Series total: 3 kΩ. Current: 12 V / 3 kΩ = 4 mA everywhere in the loop. Voltages: 4 mA × 1 kΩ = 4 V and 4 mA × 2 kΩ = 8 V (note they sum to 12 — Kirchhoff's voltage law, coming up in Module 02). Source power: 12 V × 4 mA = 48 mW.

The enabling fact: series elements carry the same current. Spotting the same-current path is the first move in reading any schematic.
