The Common-Emitter Amplifier

Ground the emitter, put a resistor in the collector, and the transistor finally does what everyone expects of it: makes a signal bigger. Then tame it. AoE 3rd ed. §2.2–2.3.

Why this matters

Module 09's emitter follower was a strange sort of amplifier: it gave you current muscle — a stiff copy of the input that could drive heavy loads — but the voltage gain was stuck at one. This module delivers the missing half. The common-emitter stage is the canonical single-transistor voltage amplifier: signal into the base, amplified (and inverted!) signal out of the collector. It is the circuit people mean when they say "a transistor amplifier," it is the front half of every op-amp you will ever use, and it introduces — in miniature — the deepest bargain in analog design: trading raw gain for predictability. Learn this stage honestly and Chapter 4's op-amps will feel less like magic and more like a very good version of something you already built.

Physics background — the transconductance viewpoint

H&H insist on a mental correction here, and it pays off for the rest of the book: the bipolar transistor is not really a current amplifier, and not really a voltage amplifier. It is a transconductance device — a voltage in controls a current out. The Ebers–Moll equation (in its useful, stripped-down form) says the collector current depends exponentially on the base–emitter voltage: IC ≈ IS·eVBE/VT, where VT = kT/q ≈ 25 mV at room temperature. An exponential is ferociously steep: every 18 mV of extra VBE doubles the collector current; 60 mV is a factor of ten.

Differentiate the exponential and you get the local steepness — the transconductance: gm = dIC/dVBE = IC/VT = IC/25 mV. At 1 mA that is 40 mA per volt. Its reciprocal is an old friend from Module 09, the intrinsic emitter resistance re = 25 mV/IC (25 Ω at 1 mA, 12.5 Ω at 2 mA).

Now the whole amplifier falls out in one sentence: a small wiggle in VBE wiggles IC by gm·ΔVBE, and a collector resistor RC converts that current wiggle back into a voltage wiggle of −gmRC·ΔVBE. Voltage → current → voltage. The minus sign is physical: more base voltage means more collector current means more drop across RC means the collector falls.

The naked CE stage — huge gain, ugly manners

Simplest version: emitter grounded, load resistor RC from the collector up to the supply, signal into the base (riding on a suitable DC bias), output taken at the collector. The gain is the transconductance argument made concrete:

G = −gmRC = −RC/re     re = 25 mV / IC grounded-emitter voltage gain; at IC = 1 mA with RC = 10 kΩ, G = −400

Gain of −400 from one transistor — impressive, and mostly useless as-is, for two connected reasons:

  • Distortion. The gain is −RC/re, but re depends on IC — and IC is exactly what the signal is wiggling. On the half-cycle where current rises, re shrinks and the gain grows; on the other half-cycle the gain droops. The output isn't a scaled copy of the input; it's a scaled copy of the input seen through a gain that changes within each cycle. Drive it with more than a few millivolts and the waveform visibly bends (widget 2 will show you).
  • Bias drift. With the emitter grounded, the quiescent current is set by VBE through that unforgiving exponential. VBE drifts about −2.1 mV/°C at fixed current — so a fixed base voltage means the current, and with it the operating point, wanders with temperature. Warm the transistor with a fingertip and watch the collector voltage walk away.

Emitter degeneration — the module's big idea

Now the fix, and it is worth reading slowly because it is the first appearance of the idea that powers the entire rest of analog electronics. Put a resistor RE between the emitter and ground. The input voltage now divides between the base–emitter junction and RE; the current wiggle it produces is ΔI ≈ Δvin/(RE + re), and RC converts it to output as before:

G = −RC / (RE + re) ≈ −RC/RE the degenerated CE stage; the approximation holds when RE ≫ re
The feedback bargain

The gain is now set by a ratio of two resistors — not by the transistor. re still wiggles with signal, but it is buried under RE: if RE = 1 kΩ and re = 17 Ω, a 20% wiggle in re moves the gain by ~0.3%. Distortion drops by the same factor; so does temperature drift of the bias point (VE is pinned near VB − 0.6, so IC = VE/RE barely cares what the transistor does).

What did it cost? Gain. −400 became −3 or −10. That is the bargain: throw away raw gain you can't trust, and buy precision, linearity, and stability you can. RE is performing negative feedback — the emitter voltage follows the input and automatically subtracts from the drive — and this exact trade, made with far more surplus gain, is what an op-amp circuit is. H&H plant the flag here; Chapter 4 waves it.

How it self-corrects, in slow motion: suppose IC tries to run too high (temperature, transistor swap, anything). More current through RE raises VE; with VB held fixed by the bias divider, that squeezes VBE, which exponentially throttles the current back down. The error fights itself. That loop — sample the output, subtract at the input — is feedback, done with one resistor.

Biasing — parking the transistor where it can swing

An amplifier spends its life around a quiescent operating point, and choosing that point is the design. The classic four-resistor bias (divider R1/R2 into the base, RE below, RC above) works because of the follower you already know: the emitter sits a diode drop below whatever the divider provides, so VE = VB − 0.6 V and IC ≈ IE = VE/RE — set by resistors, immune to β. The collector should rest near mid-supply, so the output can swing equally far up (toward the rail) and down (toward saturation) before clipping.

The design recipe, in order — each step feeds the next:

  1. Pick the quiescent current IC (1–2 mA is the comfortable default: re small, power tiny, β healthy).
  2. Choose RC to put the collector at mid-supply: RC = (VCC/2)/IC. Maximum symmetric swing.
  3. Choose RE for the gain you want: RE ≈ RC/|G| (subtract re if you're being fussy). Check that VE = ICRE comes out at least a few tenths of a volt — a volt or so is comfortable — or the bias stability you just bought evaporates.
  4. Design the divider stiff, per Module 02's 10× rule: the base looks like a load of roughly β·RE, so make the divider's Thévenin resistance no more than a tenth of that (handy shortcut: R2 ≈ β·RE/10, then R1 to place VB = VE + 0.6).
  5. Size the coupling caps per Module 05's corner rule: each cap forms a high-pass with the impedance it faces; put f3dB = 1/(2πRC) below the lowest frequency you care about.

The bypass capacitor — having it both ways

Degeneration bought stability by selling gain. The bypass capacitor buys some of the gain back without returning the stability: put a big capacitor CE across RE. At DC the cap is invisible, so the bias point is still set by RE — solid as before. But at signal frequencies the cap is a short to ground: the emitter is AC-grounded, and the stage becomes the naked CE amp again, gain −RC/re, distortion included. The gain is now frequency-dependent: low gain (−RC/RE) at DC, full gain above a corner set by the cap working against the small resistance it sees at the emitter, roughly:

fcorner ≈ 1 / (2π · re · CE) full gain above this; e.g. re = 17 Ω and CE = 100 µF → ≈ 95 Hz. Because re is small, CE must be big.

A common refinement — bypass only part of RE (two resistors in series, cap across the lower one) — keeps a chosen amount of AC degeneration and lands the gain anywhere between the two extremes. File that away; the widget below offers the all-or-nothing version.

Clipping — where the amplifier runs out of room

The output is a real voltage on a real collector, and it lives in a box. It cannot rise above the supply rail — at zero collector current the collector sits at VCC, full stop. And it cannot fall below the emitter: around VE + 0.2 V the transistor saturates (the collector–base junction forward-biases) and the output floor is reached. Ask for more swing than the box allows and the peaks simply flatten.

Asymmetric clipping is a diagnosis, not just a symptom. A well-biased stage clips both peaks at the same drive level. If one side flattens first, the quiescent collector voltage is off-center: resting too close to the rail flattens the output's top (transistor driven toward cutoff), resting too close to VE + 0.2 flattens the bottom (driven into saturation). One glance at which peak died tells you which way the bias is wrong — Task 2 makes you use it.

H&H rules of thumb
  • Degenerated gain: G = −RC/RE (more precisely −RC/(RE + re)). Naked / fully bypassed: G = −gmRC = −RC/re.
  • gm = IC/25 mV, re = 25 mV/IC: 25 Ω at 1 mA, 12.5 Ω at 2 mA. Scale by decades in your head.
  • Rest the collector at mid-supply for maximum symmetric swing; the usable window is VE + 0.2 up to VCC.
  • Bias divider 10× stiffer than the base load (Thévenin resistance ≤ β·RE/10) so the operating point doesn't depend on β.
  • The signal inverts. That minus sign is real — a rising base is a falling collector. It matters the moment you cascade stages or close a feedback loop.
Lever analogy

A common-emitter stage is a first-class lever — a seesaw with the fulcrum between effort and load:

  • The fulcrum position is RE : RC. Push the short arm (the base side, length RE) down a little and the long arm (the collector side, length RC) swings up a lot — mechanical advantage RC/RE, exactly the gain formula. Slide the fulcrum (change the ratio) and the advantage follows, without ever caring what the lever is made of.
  • The two arms move in opposite directions. Push down, the far end goes up: the inversion isn't a quirk of transistor physics, it's the geometry of the mechanism. Rising input, falling output.
  • Degeneration = lengthening the short arm. A very short input arm (small RE → the naked stage) gives spectacular advantage but every imperfection in the pivot (re's wiggling) shows up magnified. Lengthen the input arm and the pivot's quirks disappear into the geometry — less advantage, far more fidelity.

Where the analogy fails: a lever is linear forever — swing it as far as you like, the ratio holds. The transistor's lever is bolted inside a box: the long arm slams into the ceiling (VCC) and the floor (saturation), and near the extremes the pivot itself flexes (re changes with current), bending the motion before it clips.

Check yourself

Design bench

Pencil and paper, the recipe, and the anchors re = 25 mV/IC and G = −RC/RE. Check the box when you've done it and verified against the solution.

Design a common-emitter amplifier: 12 V supply, IC = 2 mA, voltage gain −10, output resting at mid-supply. Find RC, RE, the bias divider (take β = 100), and the input coupling cap for a 20 Hz corner. Run the checks: does the gain actually come out near −10, and is the divider stiff enough?

Show solution

Step 1–2, RC: mid-supply rest means 6 V across RC at 2 mA → RC = 6/0.002 = 3 kΩ (an E24 value; 3.3 kΩ from the E12 drawer rests at 5.4 V — also fine).

Step 3, RE: for |G| = 10 you want RE + re = 300 Ω. At 2 mA, re = 25/2 = 12.5 Ω, so RE = 287.5 → 300 Ω standard, giving G = −3000/312.5 = −9.6 (270 Ω would give −10.6; take your pick of which side of −10 to miss on). Check VE = 2 mA × 300 Ω = 0.6 V — on the low side of comfortable, the price of demanding gain 10 and mid-supply rest from an unbypassed stage. (The alternative: bigger RE for solid bias, bypassed down to the gain you want.)

Step 4, divider: VB = 0.6 + 0.6 = 1.2 V. Base load ≈ β·RE = 30 kΩ, so the divider Thévenin must be ≤ 3 kΩ: take R2 = 3.3 kΩ, and R1 = R2·(12 − 1.2)/1.2 = 30 kΩ. Checks: VB = 12·3.3/33.3 = 1.19 V ✓; Thévenin 30k∥3.3k = 2.97 kΩ ≤ 3 kΩ ✓; divider current 360 µA ≈ 18× the 20 µA base current ✓.

Step 5, Cin: the cap sees the stage's input impedance: R1∥R2∥β(RE+re) = 2.97k∥31.3k ≈ 2.7 kΩ. For 20 Hz: C = 1/(2π·2.7k·20) ≈ 2.9 µF → 3.3 µF (or 4.7 µF; round the corner away from the music, per Module 05).

A friend's stage: 12 V supply, RC = 4.7 kΩ, RE = 3 kΩ, divider setting VE = 1.5 V. They want ±3 V of output swing, but the scope shows the top of the output sine shaved flat while the bottom is fine. Walk the reasoning: what does a flattened top tell you about where the collector is resting? Confirm with numbers, then fix it with one resistor change.

Show solution

Read the symptom first. The output's top flattening means the collector is hitting the rail — it ran out of room going up, i.e. toward cutoff. (Remember the inversion: this happens on the input's negative peaks.) Ceiling clipping means the collector is resting too high — too little quiescent drop across RC, so too little IC.

Numbers agree: IC = 1.5/3k = 0.5 mA; rest = 12 − 0.5 mA·4.7 kΩ = 9.65 V. Upward headroom is only 2.35 V — a ±3 V request clips at the top (9.65 + 3 = 12.65 > 12) while downward there's 9.65 − 1.7 = 7.95 V to spare. Classic asymmetric clipping: the bias, not the drive, is the culprit.

Fix: pull the rest voltage down toward mid-supply by raising IC. Drop RE to 1.2 kΩ (same divider, so VE stays ≈1.5 V): IC = 1.25 mA, rest = 12 − 5.875 = 6.1 V. Headroom: 5.9 V up, 4.4 V down — ±3 V now fits with margin both ways. (Note the gain changed too, −1.6 → −3.9; if the gain was sacred you'd adjust RC and the divider together instead. Every knob touches every spec — that's amplifier design.)

One −10 stage is easy; you need gain 100 for a small microphone signal, so: two −10 stages in a row (net gain +100 — the inversions cancel). Thought-design it: what goes wrong if you wire the first collector straight to the second base (DC coupling, no cap)? Use Task 1's stage (collector resting near 6 V, RE = 300 Ω) to make it concrete. Then: what value of interstage coupling cap keeps the response flat down to 20 Hz?

Show solution

The DC-coupling disaster. Stage 2's bias recipe assumed its base sits at VB ≈ 1.2 V. Wire it to stage 1's collector and its base is dragged to ~6 V: the emitter follows to 5.4 V, and RE = 300 Ω demands IC = 5.4/300 = 18 mA — which would need 54 V across a 3 kΩ collector resistor. Impossible: the transistor slams into saturation, the collector parks at VE + 0.2 ≈ 5.6 V, and stage 2 amplifies nothing. Worse, the 18 mA also loads stage 1's bias. Two separately-perfect stages, ruined by a wire.

The cap fixes it by separating jobs: each stage keeps its own DC bias (the cap is an open at DC), while the signal — which lives above 20 Hz — sails through (Module 05's high-pass, corner below the band). The cap faces stage 2's input impedance, ≈ 3 kΩ from Task 1, so C = 1/(2π·3 kΩ·20 Hz) ≈ 2.7 µF → 4.7 µF, corner ≈ 11 Hz, safely under the band.

One honest footnote: stage 2's ~3 kΩ input impedance loads stage 1's 3 kΩ collector (they form a divider at AC), roughly halving stage 1's gain — the cascade lands nearer ×50 than ×100 unless you account for loading (Module 02, forever). Getting big, predictable gain without this bookkeeping is exactly what differential pairs and op-amps are for — next module.
