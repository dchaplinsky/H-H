Voltage Dividers, Kirchhoff & Thévenin

Two resistors, one tap point — and the theorem that lets you replace any tangle of sources and resistors with just two parts. AoE 3rd ed. §1.2.3–1.2.6.

Why this matters

The voltage divider is the most common subcircuit in all of electronics. Two resistors in series, tapped in the middle — that's it. But look closely at almost any schematic and you'll find dividers hiding everywhere: the pair of resistors biasing a transistor's base is a divider. A thermistor plus a fixed resistor reading temperature is a divider. The feedback network setting an op-amp's gain is a divider. Your oscilloscope probe is a divider (a 10:1 one, usually). If you learn one subcircuit cold — including its weakness, which is what happens when you attach something to the tap — you can read half the schematics ever drawn.

Physics background — Kirchhoff's laws

Everything in this module rests on two laws that are really just conservation laws wearing circuit clothing:

KCL — Kirchhoff's current law. The currents flowing into any node sum to zero (counting outflows as negative). This is conservation of charge: a node is just a blob of wire with essentially no capacity to store charge, so whatever flows in must immediately flow out. If 3 mA arrives and 1.2 mA leaves through one branch, exactly 1.8 mA leaves through the other — no exceptions, no delays.

KVL — Kirchhoff's voltage law. The voltage drops around any closed loop sum to zero. This is conservation of energy: voltage is energy per charge, and a charge carried around a full loop ends up exactly where it started, at the same potential energy. Whatever the battery lifts, the resistors must drop.

Both follow from electrostatics. KCL comes from charge conservation plus the fact that circuit nodes are too small to accumulate meaningful charge (that changes when we meet capacitors — on purpose). KVL comes from the electric field of static charges being conservative: the work done moving a charge between two points doesn't depend on the path, so the round trip is always zero. (Rapidly changing magnetic fields can break this — that's what a transformer exploits — but for the resistive circuits of Part I, KVL is iron law.)

The voltage divider

Take a voltage Vin, run it through R1, then R2, then to ground. The tap point between the two resistors sits at some intermediate voltage Vout. What is it?

The derivation is two lines, and both lines are Module 01 material. Nothing is connected to the tap (yet), so by KCL the same current flows through both resistors:

I = Vin / (R₁ + R₂) series resistors: one path, one current, resistances add

Vout is just the drop across R2 — that resistor's share of the total. Multiply the current by R2:

Vout = Vin · R₂ / (R₁ + R₂) the divider equation — worth engraving somewhere permanent

Notice the output depends only on the ratio of the resistors. A 1k/1k divider and a 1M/1M divider both give exactly half of Vin. So which do you build? That question has no answer until you know what the divider will be driving — which brings us to the whole point of this module.

Loading — where dividers lie to you

The divider equation assumed no current leaves through the tap. But a divider that drives nothing is a decoration. The moment you connect a load resistor RL from the tap to ground, the load sits in parallel with R2, the effective lower resistance shrinks, and Vout sags. Always down, never up: adding a resistive load can only pull the tap toward ground.

How much it sags depends on how RL compares to the divider's output impedance — the resistance the circuit appears to have when you look back into the tap. For a divider, that looking-back resistance turns out to be R1 ∥ R2 (we'll see why in a moment). A "stiff" divider — one whose output barely budges under load — is one with a low output impedance relative to whatever loads it. Stiffness costs current through the divider string, and there's the eternal trade-off: low resistances hold their voltage but burn power; high resistances sip power but fold at the first touch.

Plumbing analogy

A voltage divider is a pressure-reducing tee: high-pressure main → first constriction → tap → second constriction → drain. Water flowing through both constrictions in series drops pressure twice, and the tee between them sits at an intermediate pressure set by the ratio of the two restrictions. Cap the tap and it reads that pressure faithfully.

Now connect a fat drain pipe to the tap — a heavy load. Water gushes out the side branch, the flow through the first constriction increases, its pressure drop grows, and the pressure at the tee collapses. The gauge didn't lie about the capped pressure; you changed the circuit by measuring… er, draining it.

A stiff divider is fat pipes feeding a thin tap: the side branch draws so little compared to the main flow that the tee pressure barely notices. That's the 10× rule in plumbing form — keep the tap's draw small next to the through-flow.

Thévenin's theorem — the great simplifier

Here is one of the most useful facts in circuit theory. Any two-terminal network built from voltage sources, current sources, and resistors — however baroque — behaves, as seen from its two terminals, exactly like a single voltage source VTh in series with a single resistor RTh. Not approximately. Exactly, for any load you attach.

Finding the two magic numbers takes two thought experiments:

  • VTh = the open-circuit voltage. Attach nothing and measure the terminal voltage. With no load current, there's no drop across RTh, so the terminals show the bare source.
  • RTh = VTh / Ishort-circuit. Short the terminals and measure the current (mentally, please — real circuits object). Equivalently, and usually easier: set every voltage source to zero (replace with a wire) and every current source to zero (replace with an open), then compute the resistance seen looking into the terminals.

For the divider, both experiments are one-liners. Open-circuit voltage is just the divider equation. And with the source shorted, look back into the tap: you see R1 going to ground (through the dead source) and R2 going to ground — in parallel:

VTh = Vin · R₂/(R₁+R₂)      RTh = R₁ ∥ R₂ = R₁R₂/(R₁+R₂) the Thévenin equivalent of a voltage divider — this pair is used constantly

Why care? Because the equivalent makes loading calculations trivial. A 10 V supply with a 10k/10k divider becomes a 5 V source in series with 5 kΩ. Attach any load and the loaded voltage is just another divider: VTh · RL/(RL + RTh). Hang 5 kΩ on it and you get 2.5 V — the messy "parallel combination back into the original divider" arithmetic collapses to one line. The Thévenin resistance is the output impedance we met above; the two phrases name the same number.

Norton duality, briefly: the same theorem comes in a current-source flavor. Any such network is also equivalent to a current source IN = VTh/RTh in parallel with the same RTh. Our 5 V/5 kΩ example is equally a 1 mA source paralleled by 5 kΩ. Same terminal behavior, different mental furniture; Thévenin suits voltage-ish thinking, Norton suits current-ish thinking (transistor collectors, photodiodes). Convert freely.

H&H rule of thumb — the 10× rule

For a divider (or any source) to be usably "stiff", make the load impedance at least 10× the source's output impedance. With RL = 10·RTh, the output sags by 1/11 ≈ 9% — usually tolerable, often negligible next to resistor tolerances.

The same habit, cascaded through a design: each stage should look into a load ≥10× its own output impedance, so stages don't drag each other down and you can design them one at a time. Corollary in the other direction: a good measuring instrument should present an input impedance ≥10× (preferably 100×+) the impedance of the node it probes — otherwise the act of measuring rewrites the answer.

Potentiometers — the adjustable divider

A potentiometer is a voltage divider you can turn: one resistive element, three terminals, with the middle one (the wiper) sliding along the element. Wherever the wiper sits, the element is split into an R1 above and an R2 below with R1 + R2 constant — a divider with a knob for the ratio. Volume controls, contrast trimmers, joystick axes: all pots.

Everything in this module applies. A 10k pot set to mid-travel has RTh = 5k ∥ 5k = 2.5 kΩ — its worst case, at the middle. Load a pot with too small an RL and not only does the output sag, the sag varies with the setting, so the dial's response goes lumpy and nonlinear. The 10× rule applied against the pot's worst-case RTh (one quarter of the total element resistance) keeps the knob honest.

Check yourself

Design bench

Pencil, paper, and the divider equation. Check the box once you've worked it and compared with the solution.

Design a divider that produces 3.3 V from a 5 V supply, with an output impedance of about 10 kΩ. Give R1 and R2 as standard values, then compute the current the divider draws continuously and comment on the trade-off you just made.

Show solution

Two constraints, two unknowns. Ratio: R2/(R1+R2) = 3.3/5 = 0.66. Output impedance: R1∥R2 ≈ 10 kΩ. A slick way to see it: RTh = R1∥R2 can be written R1 × (ratio), so R1 = 10k/0.66 ≈ 15.2 kΩ, and R2 = R1·(3.3/1.7) ≈ 29.4 kΩ.

Standard values: R1 = 15 kΩ, R2 = 30 kΩ — and this pair is a small miracle: 5 V × 30/45 = 3.333 V, and 15k∥30k = 10 kΩ exactly. (30 kΩ is E24; if your drawer only has E12, 15k/33k gives 3.44 V and 10.3 kΩ — ask whether 4% high matters before reaching for precision parts.)

Drain: 5 V / 45 kΩ ≈ 111 µA, always, whether anyone uses the 3.3 V or not. The trade-off: scale both resistors down 10× and the output gets 10× stiffer (1 kΩ) but drains 1.1 mA — real money on a coin cell. Scale up 10× and you sip 11 µA but fold under any load below ~1 MΩ. Output impedance is a design choice, not an afterthought.

A 12 V source feeds a divider of R1 = 4.7 kΩ over R2 = 1 kΩ. Find the Thévenin equivalent of the tap, then use it — and only it — to predict the output voltage with a 1 kΩ load attached. Check your answer the long way if you're suspicious.

Show solution

Open-circuit: VTh = 12 × 1/(4.7+1) = 12/5.7 ≈ 2.11 V. Looking back in with the source shorted: RTh = 4.7k∥1k = 4700/5.7 ≈ 825 Ω.

Now the 1 kΩ load sees a plain series pair — one more divider: V = 2.105 × 1000/(1000+825) ≈ 1.15 V.

Long way: 1k∥1k = 500 Ω, so V = 12 × 500/(4700+500) = 12 × 500/5200 = 1.154 V. Same answer, as the theorem promised — but notice the Thévenin route required no revisiting of the original circuit. That's the point: characterize once, load repeatedly. Also note the brutal sag (2.11 V → 1.15 V, about 45%): a 1 kΩ load on an 825 Ω source violates the 10× rule by nearly a factor of ten.

A sensor behaves like a voltage source with a 100 kΩ output impedance (its Thévenin resistance). You wire it straight into an ADC input that looks like 10 kΩ to ground. What fraction of the sensor's true voltage does the ADC actually see? Quantify the error, then propose the fix.

Show solution

Thévenin makes this a one-line divider: the ADC sees V × 10k/(100k+10k) = V × 1/11 ≈ 9.1% of the true voltage — a 91% error. Not a subtle calibration issue; the reading is garbage. (The 10× rule, held up to a mirror: here the load is 10× smaller than the source impedance, so the divider ratio ≈ 1/11 instead of ≈ 1.)

You can't fix it with resistors — any resistive network between a weak source and a hungry load just makes another lossy divider. What you need is a circuit with a high input impedance and a low output impedance: a buffer (unity-gain follower). An op-amp follower presents ~GΩ to the sensor (drawing essentially nothing) and ~ohms to the ADC (sagging essentially never). Impedance transformation without touching the voltage is one of the two great things amplifiers do for a living — the op-amp modules will make good on this IOU.
