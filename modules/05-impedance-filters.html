Impedance, Reactance & Passive Filters

Stop asking what a circuit does to a voltage step and start asking what it does to each frequency — the viewpoint shift that turns one resistor and one capacitor into a filter. AoE 3rd ed. §1.7.

Why this matters

Everything so far lived in the time domain: flip a switch, watch an exponential. This module makes the one genuine conceptual leap of Chapter 1 — moving to the frequency domain, where you describe a circuit not by what it does to a step but by what it does to each sine wave you could feed it. It sounds like a mere change of bookkeeping. It isn't. It's the difference between staring at a wiggly scope trace and saying "this passes the bass and kills the hiss." Every coupling capacitor, every tone control, every anti-alias filter in front of an ADC, every scope probe on your bench is exactly the circuit in this module — a resistor and a capacitor arranged as a frequency-dependent voltage divider. Learn to read it once and you'll recognize it, disguised, in half of all schematics ever drawn.

Physics background

Why sine waves, of all shapes? Because linear circuits — anything built from R, L and C — treat them with unique courtesy: drive a linear circuit with a sine wave and the steady-state answer at every node is a sine wave of the same frequency. Only two things can happen to it: its amplitude changes, and it shifts in time (phase). A square wave in gives some rounded mess out; a sine in gives a sine out, always. That's Fourier's great idea in one paragraph: since any signal can be written as a sum of sines, and a linear circuit processes each sine independently (scale it, shift it, sum the results), the entire behavior of a circuit is captured by two curves — gain versus frequency and phase versus frequency. Characterize the sines and you've characterized everything.

We'll write frequency two ways: f in hertz (cycles per second), and the angular frequency ω = 2πf in radians per second — the same information with the 2π pre-paid, which unclutters the formulas.

Reactance is not resistance. A resistor turns electrical energy into heat, irreversibly, at any frequency. A capacitor (or inductor) instead stores energy for part of each cycle and hands it back during the rest — the field charges up, then discharges, twice per cycle, dissipating nothing (ideally). The opposition it presents to AC is called reactance, and because it comes from energy storage rather than friction, it depends on frequency and it shifts the current a quarter-cycle away from the voltage. Same units as resistance — ohms — completely different personality.

Reactance — opposition that depends on frequency

How hard does a capacitor fight an AC current? Recall Module 03's law, I = C·dV/dt. A fast-wiggling voltage has a huge dV/dt, so it drives a big current through the same capacitor — the capacitor fights less as frequency rises. Do the calculus on V = V₀sin(ωt) and the ratio of voltage amplitude to current amplitude comes out to:

XC = 1 / (2πfC) = 1 / ωC capacitive reactance, in ohms — falls as frequency rises

Get a feel for the numbers. A 1 µF capacitor at 50 Hz (mains hum) presents about 3.2 kΩ; the same part at 10 kHz presents about 16 Ω — two hundred times less. To DC (f = 0) it presents infinite ohms: an open circuit, as Module 03 promised. To a fast enough signal it's nearly a piece of wire. One component, every resistance value from zero to infinity — you just have to ask at what frequency.

Inductors (Module 04) mirror this exactly: XL = 2πfL, rising with frequency. An inductor is a short at DC and an open at high frequency — the capacitor's temperamental opposite. Between them they let you steer any frequency anywhere, which is the whole art of filter design.

Impedance — resistance, generalized

Once reactances enter a circuit you need a slightly bigger idea than resistance: impedance (symbol Z, ohms) — the general ratio of voltage to current, carrying two numbers instead of one: a magnitude (how much opposition) and a phase (how far current is shifted in time relative to voltage).

  • A resistor's current is perfectly in step with its voltage: phase 0°.
  • A capacitor's current leads its voltage by 90° — current must flow first for voltage to build up (I = C·dV/dt again).
  • An inductor's current lags by 90°.

Because of those 90° angles, series resistance and reactance don't add lengthwise — they add like the sides of a right triangle:

|Z| = √(R² + X²) magnitude of a series R + C (or R + L) combination; the phase angle is atan(X/R)

H&H (§1.7.5) do this properly with complex numbers — impedance becomes a single complex quantity Z = R + jX and all the algebra of Ohm's law survives intact. It's an elegant machine and worth learning soon. For this module, though, magnitudes plus the two 90° facts will take us everywhere we need to go, and keep the intuition in view the whole way.

The RC low-pass — a voltage divider that changes its mind

Here is the punchline of the module. Take Module 02's voltage divider and replace the lower resistor with a capacitor: series R, then C to ground, output across the C. At low frequency the capacitor's reactance is enormous — the lower leg of the divider is huge, so essentially the full input appears at the output. At high frequency XC shrivels — the lower leg approaches a short and the output collapses. It is a voltage divider whose lower leg shrinks with frequency. That's the entire theory of the RC low-pass filter; everything else is bookkeeping.

The bookkeeping (using |Z| for the divider ratio): gain = XC/√(R² + XC²) = 1/√(1 + (f/f3dB)²), where the crossover — the frequency at which R and XC are equal — is:

f3dB = 1 / (2πRC) the corner, breakpoint, or cutoff frequency — where R = XC

At exactly f3dB the gain is 1/√2 ≈ 70.7% — not the ½ you might guess from "equal legs," because the two legs are 90° apart and divide like triangle sides. Since power goes as voltage squared, 70.7% of the voltage is exactly half the power — which is what "−3 dB" means, as we'll see below. Beyond the corner, the gain falls by half for every doubling of frequency: 6 dB per octave, equivalently 20 dB per decade. A gentle slope — one RC can only do so much.

The RC high-pass — the same divider, flipped

Swap the two components: series C, then R to ground, output across the R. Now the upper leg is the one that shrinks with frequency. At DC the capacitor is an open circuit and nothing gets through — this is the "coupling capacitor" of Module 03's bench, now with its theory attached. At high frequency the cap is a wire and the signal sails through untouched. Same corner formula, mirror-image response: gain = (f/f3dB)/√(1 + (f/f3dB)²). The circuit passes everything above f3dB, blocks DC completely, and rolls off below the corner at the same 6 dB/octave. When you size a coupling cap "so the corner is below the lowest frequency you care about," this is the curve you're positioning.

Decibels — the logarithm you'll use daily

Filter responses span factors of thousands, so engineers quote voltage ratios logarithmically:

dB = 20 · log₁₀(V / V₀) the 20 (not 10) is because power goes as V², and decibels were born as power ratios

Memorize this small ladder and most filter talk becomes mental arithmetic:

dBVoltage ratioPower ratioRead as
0 dB×1×1unchanged
−3 dB×0.707×½the corner — half power
−6 dB×0.5×¼one octave past the corner
−20 dB×0.1×0.01one decade past the corner

And because it's a logarithm, cascaded stages simply add their dB — two −20 dB stages make −40 dB. (Whether they actually behave that way when connected depends on loading — see the rules below.)

H&H rules of thumb
  • f3dB = 1/(2πRC). Anchor: 1 kΩ and 1 µF give 159 Hz — call it "160 Hz" and scale by decades from there (1 kΩ & 1 nF → 160 kHz; 10 kΩ & 1 µF → 16 Hz).
  • One RC stage rolls off at 6 dB/octave = 20 dB/decade. No more, no less. Need a steeper skirt? You need more stages, or an inductor, or an op-amp — not a different R and C.
  • At f3dB the phase shift is 45° (lagging for a low-pass, leading for a high-pass), heading toward 90° far past the corner and 0° far before it.
  • Load a filter with ≥10× its impedance — Module 02's divider rule, now frequency-flavored. A load comparable to R drags the corner and the passband gain around; ten-to-one keeps the errors in the few-percent bin.
Suspension analogy

A car's suspension is a mechanical filter, and it runs both filters at once:

  • Low-pass = the car body. A heavy body on soft springs follows the slow undulations of the road — hills, dips, gentle swells — and simply cannot respond to gravel buzz; the shaking is absorbed before it reaches your coffee. Slow components pass, fast ones are smoothed away. Softer springs or a heavier body = a bigger "RC" = a lower corner.
  • High-pass = the wheels. Watch a wheel instead: it hammers up and down over every pebble (fast components faithfully followed) while staying, on average, a constant height off the road — the slow hill-climbing is invisible in the wheel-to-body distance. The wheels "pass the gravel and block the hills."
  • A capacitor to ground is a pressure cushion. Plumbers hang a small air-filled tank (a water-hammer arrestor) on a pipe: sudden pressure spikes compress the air pocket and vanish — the tank swallows fast ripples. But a slow trend, like mains pressure drifting over an hour, just refills the tank to a new level and marches on. Fast wiggles absorbed, slow ones ignored: that's a decoupling capacitor in copper and air.

Where the analogies fail: a real suspension has damping and a resonance — it can bounce, which a plain RC never does (that needs an inductor's worth of "mass"; see Module 04). And the cushion tank saturates — compress the air pocket fully and it stops working — while an ideal capacitor scales linearly forever.

Check yourself

Design bench

Pencil, paper, and the 1 kΩ + 1 µF = 159 Hz anchor. Check the box when you've done it and verified against the solution.

A turntable preamp picks up subsonic "rumble" — warped-record and bearing wobble below 20 Hz that wastes amplifier power and flaps woofer cones. Design a high-pass to cut below 20 Hz, using the next stage's 47 kΩ input impedance as the filter's R (a series cap into that input — sound familiar?). Pick C, then the nearest standard value, and say what the standard value does to the corner.

Show solution

Solve the corner formula for C: C = 1/(2πRf) = 1/(2π × 47 kΩ × 20 Hz) ≈ 169 nF. No such drawer exists, so pick a neighbor — and here the direction matters, so think before rounding:

180 nF (E12) → f3dB ≈ 18.8 Hz. Corner slides slightly below 20 Hz: a touch less rumble rejection, bass fully intact. 220 nF → 15.4 Hz: even gentler on bass, weaker on rumble. 150 nF → 22.6 Hz: strongest rumble cut, but now the corner sits inside the audio band and 20 Hz program material is already −3 dB down.

For a rumble filter the priority is rejection, and a 6 dB/octave skirt is lazy anyway, so 150–180 nF is the defensible zone; 180 nF is the safe pick. The transferable lesson: a single RC corner is a soft boundary — decide which side of the spec must not suffer, and round the corner away from it.

A sensor line carries a wanted signal that lives below 1 kHz, contaminated with 50 kHz hash from a nearby switching regulator. Design an RC low-pass with f3dB ≈ 5 kHz. One catch: the sensor itself has 1 kΩ of source impedance, which sits in series with whatever R you add — include it. Then compute the attenuation at 50 kHz and the droop at 1 kHz. Is the wanted signal harmed?

Show solution

The R you add isn't the R the filter sees. Total series resistance = Radded + 1 kΩ. Pick Radded = 2.2 kΩ (standard), total 3.2 kΩ. Then C = 1/(2π × 3.2 kΩ × 5 kHz) ≈ 9.9 nF → 10 nF standard, giving f3dB ≈ 4.97 kHz. (Skipping the source impedance would have put the corner at 7.2 kHz — a 45% miss from a "hidden" resistor. Thévenin never stops being useful.)

At 50 kHz: f/fc ≈ 10, so gain ≈ 1/√(1+100) ≈ 0.099 → −20 dB — one decade above the corner, 20 dB down, exactly as the slope rule promises. The hash drops to a tenth.

At 1 kHz: f/fc ≈ 0.2, gain = 1/√(1.04) ≈ 0.98 → −0.17 dB. The wanted signal loses 2% — inaudible, unmeasurable in most contexts. That's the classic single-RC bargain: park the corner ~5× above the signal and ~10× below the trash, and both sides are happy. If a tenth of the hash is still too much, cascade another stage or reach for an LC.

A ×10 scope probe is a 9 MΩ series resistor feeding the scope's input: 1 MΩ in parallel with ~15 pF of unavoidable cable-plus-input capacitance. At DC that's a clean 10:1 divider. Compute what the 15 pF does at higher frequencies — where's the corner of the uncompensated divider, and what would a 1 kHz square wave look like? Then explain the small trimmer capacitor across the 9 MΩ: what condition makes the divider frequency-independent?

Show solution

The uncompensated disaster. Seen from the capacitor, the Thévenin resistance is 9 MΩ ∥ 1 MΩ = 900 kΩ, so the divider has a corner at f = 1/(2π × 900 kΩ × 15 pF) ≈ 11.8 kHz. Below it you get the intended ÷10; above it the 15 pF shorts out the 1 MΩ and the response keeps falling. Your "×10 probe" is secretly a 12 kHz low-pass — hopeless for a scope. A 1 kHz square wave (edges full of harmonics far above 12 kHz) would show sagging, rounded corners: each edge arrives as an RC exponential with τ = 900 kΩ × 15 pF ≈ 13.5 µs.

The fix. Put a capacitor C₁ across the 9 MΩ so both legs of the divider are an R∥C pair. The divider stops caring about frequency when both legs have the same corner — the condition R₁C₁ = R₂C₂. At low frequency the resistors set the ratio (1/10); at high frequency the capacitors set it (C's divide as C₂/(C₁+C₂) — reactances are inverse in C), and R₁C₁ = R₂C₂ makes both ratios identical, so every frequency divides by 10. Here: C₁ = R₂C₂/R₁ = 1 MΩ × 15 pF / 9 MΩ ≈ 1.7 pF — adjustable, because the scope's 15 pF isn't precise.

Why a trimmer: mis-set it and the square wave tells on you — C₁ too big overshoots the edges (highs over-delivered), too small rounds them (highs starved). That's exactly what the "probe compensation" square-wave terminal on every scope is for, and now you know what you're adjusting: making R₁C₁ equal R₂C₂.
